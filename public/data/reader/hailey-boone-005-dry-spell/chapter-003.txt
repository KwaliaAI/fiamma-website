By half past nine on Saturday Eli had fourteen acres of the south piece on the ground and a hydraulic fitting on the header that had gone from weeping to dripping, and he had told himself at five that he was not going near the barn before noon.

He had also told himself he was not going to think about a red line on a whiteboard. *IT'S AN AISLE.* He had gone to bed on it and gotten up on it, and he had cut fourteen acres with it riding in the cab where the dog used to. She was wrong. It was hay. He had stood in the middle of it at dusk on Friday looking at the creek through her eyes, and she was a little bit right, and he held that against her more than the wrong part.

The fitting was in the coffee can on the office desk. He drove the pickup back for it with the window down, and he counted four vehicles in the yard that were not his. There was a florist's van with the back doors open, a white SUV, a pickup with a tent company's name on the door, and her rental, parked one space left of the dog.

Garbage was walking the line of them. She passed the van's tires, and the SUV's, and the tent man's, at the same unhurried walk, and gave none of them so much as a look.

*"Day visitors,"* Eli said. The hen went on around the corner of the barn.

He stopped inside the big door, where the light changed, and nobody saw him.

She had five people on the main floor in the space the swather had left. She was walking backward in front of them with the binder open on one arm, talking with the other hand, and none of the five was looking at the tractor. They were looking where she pointed. *"Bar on this wall, because at six the sun comes through that gap and every drink on it lights up. Band there, under the loft. Dolores, your pass is the side door, your ovens are outside under the fly, nobody carries a tray across a dance floor."* She stepped back into one of her own blue tape circles without looking down for it. *"This is table one. The bride's grandmother sits here and she can see the door, the loft, and the cake without turning her chair."*

He had watched men sort cattle who were worse at it. She knew where every one of them was standing. When the tent man drifted, she had him back with a question before he got to the baler. Eli stood in the door with his hat in his hand and forgot what he had come for.

*"And the equipment,"* the woman called Dolores said. She was the caterer, by the clogs. *"That's staying?"*

*"That's a working ranch,"* Lena said. *"It's out by Thursday."*

He had not told her Thursday. Nobody had told her Thursday. She said it with her whole face, pleasant and sure, and he saw the caterer write it down.

The tractor started.

It was at the back of the floor with the baler on it, and the kid was in the cab. Dalton was seventeen and worked for him from six to six, and he drove a tractor the way he did everything else, at a walk, carefully, straight at whatever was in front of him. He brought it up the middle of the barn. He brought the baler behind it. The five vendors moved to the walls in good order, the florist with a bucket of something pink held up out of the way, and the tractor rolled over circles nine, five, and one, and the baler followed it over the bride's grandmother, and Dalton lifted one hand to everybody as he went out the door.

Eli caught up with him at the pole building.

*"What are you doing."*

*"Clearing the floor."* Dalton shut it off. *"It was on the board. Ten o'clock, main floor clear. I figured you two worked it out."*

*"That was in red."*

*"So?"*

*"I write in black."*

Dalton thought about that. *"Nobody told me there was colors."*

*"There's colors. You work for the black one."*

*"Okay. You want it back in?"*

Eli looked at the barn. Through the door he could see her bent over with a roll of blue tape, putting table one back down, with the caterer holding the end of it.

*"Leave it,"* he said. *"You'll want it out for the rake tomorrow anyhow."*

*"That's what I thought,"* Dalton said, and it plainly was not.

---

The tent man's pickup and the SUV were pulling out when he came back for the fitting. The florist was still there, up in the loft with a tape measure, and Lena was in his office.

She was standing in front of the board with her back to the door. She had the red marker, and under *MAIN FLOOR CLEAR* and his *CLEAR OF WHAT* she had drawn one check mark.

*"Thank Dalton for me,"* she said, without turning around.

*"Dalton can't read a board."*

*"Dalton reads beautifully."*

He came in. The office was eight feet by ten with a cot and a desk in it, and with two people there was not much left over. He reached past her for the coffee can, and she did not step aside, and he got the fitting out of it with his arm an inch from her ribs.

*"The tractor's back Monday,"* he said.

*"No."*

*"That's my word."*

*"It's a good word. I see why you like it."*

He took the black marker and wrote it under her check mark. *TRACTOR BACK MON.* She wrote *NO.* under that, in red, small. He looked at the two of them side by side, his own word in her hand. The fan in the window was not running and the room smelled of marker and of her, soap and sun and something green she'd had her hands in.

*"Thursday,"* he said.

*"What about it."*

*"You told that woman I'm out by Thursday."*

*"She needed a day."*

*"So you gave her one of mine."*

*"I gave her a day I can move. If I'd said I don't know, she'd have been on the phone to Chicago before she was off the drive."* She capped the marker and uncapped it again. *"You're going to tell me that's the same thing I did Thursday on the phone."*

*"I wasn't going to tell you anything."*

*"You were going to stand there."*

*"Yeah."*

She turned around then. It put her back to the board and her face a foot under his. There was blue tape stuck to her wrist and a red line across the heel of her hand.

*"The house forty,"* he said.

*"I promised it to her. The bride. Yesterday at three."*

*"Before you read the map or after."*

*"Before."* She did not look away to say it. *"I read the map about four minutes after. Then I came in here and wrote it down, because you were going to find a photographer standing in it on Monday, and I'd sooner you heard it from the board."*

*"I saw your aisle."*

*"I'm sorry about the aisle."*

*"It stood back up."*

*"Did it?"*

*"Most of it."*

Up in the loft the florist's tape measure rattled back into its case. Lena's eyes went to the question mark, the one in two colors, and came back to him.

*"Why is that a question,"* she said. *"Everything else on your side has a day."*

*"Because I don't know yet."*

*"You don't know."*

*"It's five days from ready and the water's been off it since Sunday. I'm letting the ground firm up so I can get a swather on it. Where it is by Thursday depends on what the week does."*

*"So ask the week to wait till the twenty-second."*

*"It's alfalfa, Lena."*

Her chin came up.

*"Then write that,"* she said. *"Write something I can plan around. I can plan around anything. I cannot plan around a punctuation mark."*

She reached back with the heel of her hand to wipe the question mark off, and he caught her wrist.

He had only meant to stop her. He had her wrist, and her pulse was going under his thumb faster than her voice had been, and he stood there holding it and did not let go.

*"You've got marker on you,"* he said.

*"I know what I've got on me."*

*"Lena."*

*"Let go or don't,"* she said. *"I have a florist."*

He didn't. She put her free hand on the side of his neck, flat, with her thumb under his jaw, and he came down to her or she brought him, and he would not have sworn to which.

Her mouth was hot and tasted of motel coffee, and it opened under his at once. She kissed the way she ran a floor, like she knew where everything was. He let go of her wrist and put that hand on the back of her neck, on the place he had been looking at since Thursday, and the skin there was damp and the hair came down over his knuckles. She made a low sound into his mouth. He stepped in, and her shoulders went back against the whiteboard, and the whole board knocked once against the wall.

*"Quiet,"* she said, against his jaw.

*"That was you."*

*"That was your board."*

Her hand came off his neck and both of them went under his shirt. They were cool on his back, and then they were not cool. She ran them up his spine to the shoulder blades and pulled, and he had his thigh between hers and his other hand flat on the board beside her head, and he could feel the marker tray against his hip and did not care about it. She bit his lower lip, lightly enough that it could have been an accident. Then she did it again.

*"Okay,"* he said, into her mouth.

*"Yeah?"*

*"Yeah."*

He kissed her throat, where the pulse was. She tipped her head back to give it to him and it hit the board, and she laughed, one breath, and he felt the laugh under his mouth. Her leg came up along the outside of his. He got his hand under her knee.

An air horn went off in the yard.

It went twice, long, like a horn that had come a distance and expected to be met. Clutch barked once. From the loft, down the whole length of the barn, the florist called, *"Lena? Hon? Is that for you?"*

Neither of them moved for a second. He had her knee in his hand. She had ten fingers in his back.

*"I don't have a truck today,"* she said, to his collarbone.

*"Somebody does."*

She got her hands out of his shirt. She fixed her hair with two movements that had worked for her before, picked the binder up off the desk, and looked at him once. It was a short look, with no plan in it, and he liked it better than anything she had said all morning.

Then she went out through the office door, and he saw her back.

Her shirt was white. Across the shoulders of it, backward, in black, was most of *NORTH. Cut Fri 13.* Beside it in red, also backward, was *SAT 14. 10 AM.* and at about the height of her belt, small and perfectly clear, was the word *NO.*

*"Lena,"* he said. *"Your shirt."*

*"I know, it's linen,"* she said, and she was gone across the floor.

---

It was a box truck, twenty-six feet, with the rental company's name on the side and a driver already down out of it with a tablet.

*"Mercer?"* he said. *"Forty tables."*

*"Monday,"* Lena said. Her mouth felt like someone else's. Her knees were not entirely with her either, and there was a place on her throat that she could feel the air on. *"I'm Monday. I have you Monday the sixteenth between ten and two."*

*"I got you today."* He turned the tablet around. *"We had a cancellation in Big Sky and dispatch routed you early. They called the number on the account to confirm."*

*"What number."*

He read it out. It had a 312 area code and it was the front desk at Corinne's.

*"That's not my number."*

*"It's the number on the account."*

Behind her the florist had come down out of the loft and was loading her buckets into the van, slowly, with her head turned. Bea had driven in from Livingston for a walk-through and had gotten a tractor in it. Dolores's SUV was still in the drive too, stopped halfway down with the brake lights on, because Dolores had seen the truck in her mirror and wanted to know.

*"I can take them back,"* the driver said. *"No charge, it's our mistake. But I'm in Billings Monday and West Yellowstone Tuesday, and the next truck coming down this valley is Thursday."*

*"Thursday the nineteenth."*

*"Yes, ma'am."*

Thursday was the day Nora Brennan walked into this barn. The tables went down Wednesday, and the photographer wanted the main floor empty on Monday.

He stepped out of the barn behind her. She knew it without turning. Her skin had learned him in the last ten minutes and was not going to unlearn him for a truck. He came across the yard with the fitting in his hand. He passed four feet from her and looked at the truck, and at the tablet, and at her, in that order, and he kept walking. His shirt was out at the back where her hands had been.

She could have stopped him with his name. *It's your bay. Can I.* She had the hay bay in the corner of her eye, swept to the boards, with its side door standing open, and she had Bea and Dolores watching to see what a planner did when a truck came two days early to a barn with a baler in the yard. He opened the door of his pickup.

She should have sent the truck back and found another. Failing that she should have said one word before that door shut.

The door shut.

*"No, this is fine,"* she said. *"This actually helps me."* She took the tablet and signed it with her finger, and her finger was not steady, and the signature came out looking like someone else's. *Lena Mercer.* It was the first thing in Montana she had signed in her own name alone.

*"Where do you want them."*

*"Through there,"* she said, and pointed at the hay bay.

He had a pallet jack and he was fast. Forty tables went across the yard in six stacks and in through the side door, onto a floor that somebody had swept that week with some care. Lena stood in the sun and initialed for each stack and did not count one of them. She was listening to a pickup go south down the field road. When she could not hear it anymore she found she had two fingers against her own mouth, and she took them away.

The driver looked around on his third trip. He looked at the flatbed, and at the tractor and baler out by the pole building, and at the stack of pallets against the bay wall.

*"You got a hay operation going in your venue?"*

*"It's a working ranch. It's seasonal."*

*"It's a baler,"* he said, kindly enough, and made a note.

Dolores's brake lights went off and she drove on. Bea shut the van doors and came over, wiping her hands.

*"I'll have the trial bouquet to you Monday morning,"* she said. *"Peonies are a no in August, I told the mother, so it's garden roses and that lavender. Hon, you've got something on your back."*

*"What?"*

*"On your shirt. It's writing."*

Lena went to the truck's side mirror and turned her shoulder to it. It took her a second to read, because the mirror turned it the right way around again. *NORTH. Cut Fri 13.* And lower, in her own neat red, *NO.*

*"Is that a schedule?"* Bea said.

*"It's a whiteboard."*

*"Well, it's all over you."*

*"I leaned on it."*

*"Mm-hm,"* Bea said. She looked at the office window, and then she looked at Lena's mouth, and then she got in her van, a woman with a good piece of news and the whole drive home to enjoy it.

---

The truck had gone and so had the pickup. Lena could hear the swather again, to the south this time. She stood in the door of the hay bay and counted the tables, which she had not done while they came off the truck. There were forty. In the corner, on top of the stacked eights, the hen had settled herself with her feathers out.

*"You don't live here either,"* Lena told her.

She waited for him. She changed her shirt in the car first, for the gray one out of her gym bag. Then she set fourteen rounds of tape again, and called the tent man back about the fly, and called the rental company, where a recording told her that the office was open Monday through Friday. She did not call Corinne's front desk to ask what they had said to dispatch. It was Saturday, and nobody would be on the desk.

She ate a sleeve of crackers sitting in the gravel beside the dog. The dog put her chin on Lena's boot. It was a hundred and forty guests, forty tables, one generator at eleven hundred dollars, and seven days, and for about fifteen minutes Lena sat in the dust in Montana and thought about none of it. She thought about a hand on the back of her neck. It had been a big hand and it had not been in any hurry, and it had held on like a man steadying something he meant to keep upright.

*"That's enough of that,"* she said. The dog did not think so.

At six the kid went home. At ten past seven the swather came up the lane and stopped at the fuel tank.

She was in the office when he walked in. She had already written it. She had wanted to say it out loud, and had practiced, and then she had seen the board and the smear across the middle of it where her shoulders had been, with half of *NORTH* gone and his Friday wiped to a gray cloud. She had picked up the red marker because it was easier.

*40 TABLES. HAY BAY. THEY CAME EARLY.*

He read it. He had dust in his eyebrows and a burn across the back of his neck that was new today. He did not look at her mouth, and she watched him not do it.

*"Truck was yours,"* he said.

*"Monday's truck. They rerouted it."*

*"You could've sent it back."*

*"Yes."*

*"You signed for it."*

*"Yes."*

*"Why."*

There were three true answers. She gave him the worst one. *"Because two vendors were watching, and I wanted to look like someone who knew what she was doing."*

He nodded slowly. She had expected him to say something about it, and he let it stand.

*"I should have asked you first,"* she said. *"I'm asking now. It's your bay."*

*"It is."*

*"How long can I have it."*

He went past her to the board, close, without touching her, and she felt him go by all down her right side. He picked up the black marker and wrote one word under her line and underlined it.

*WED.*

*"Wednesday I lay pallets,"* he said. *"The house forty goes in there and nothing else does. It's the only hay I put under a roof."*

She heard that. She had *DO NOT CUT* a foot from his hand in her own capitals.

*"It goes in there when,"* she said.

*"When it's cut and cured and baled."*

*"And it's cut when."*

He looked at the question mark. *"Thursday at the soonest."*

*"Thursday she walks in here. Saturday she walks down it."*

*"I know what Saturday is."*

*"I wrote do not cut."*

*"I read it."*

Nobody had moved. The fan was still off. Lena could hear the dog's tail on the gravel outside, sweeping once and stopping.

*"So I have until Thursday to change your mind,"* she said.

*"You've got till Thursday to change the weather."*

*"And if the day changes?"*

*"Then you hear it from me."* He tipped the marker at the board. *"Before you read it up there. You said you can't plan around a question mark."*

*"I can plan around that."*

*"Wednesday for the tables."*

*"Wednesday."*

*"I'm not carrying them out."*

*"I didn't ask you to carry them."*

*"All right."*

*"All right,"* she said.

Nobody said anything about the morning. It was in the room regardless, eight feet by ten of it, and it was on the board in gray.

*"Bea's going to tell Livingston,"* Lena said.

*"Bea'll tell Bozeman."*

*"What's she going to tell them?"*

*"That I've got writing on a wedding planner."*

*"You do not."*

*"Had,"* he said. *"You changed your shirt."*

So he had looked. She had been watching for his eyes on her mouth and he had been reading her shoulders. She held the red marker in both hands and found that she had nothing to add in either color.

He was looking at the board. So was she. He rewrote *NORTH. Cut Fri 13. Bale Mon.* at the top, in the clean space above where it had been, and he left the smear alone. It was about as wide as her shoulders.
