Lena slept three hours and ten minutes on Monday morning, and woke up in number six with hay in her bra, a blister across each palm, and a photographer due at four.

She had carried eleven bales. She counted them again in the shower, with her hands held up out of the water because the hot stung. Eleven, in his gloves, before Dalton had said very politely that he could maybe get the rest. Eli had not said anything. He had gone back up into the cab and started the baler, and when she drove out the gate he had lifted two fingers off the wheel. Then she put on a clean shirt, the right way out, checked it twice in the mirror, and drove back to the barn with the binder buckled in.

The flatbed came past her in the yard at ten with a load on, eight bales high. Dalton was driving. Eli was standing on top of the stack with a hay hook in his hand, and Clutch was lying across the cab roof. He lifted the hand with the hook in it. It was the hand a man lifts to the mail carrier.

*"Morning,"* he said.

*"Morning."*

*"North's up."*

*"I heard."*

Dalton leaned out the window. He had the careful face of a boy who had been told nothing and had worked all of it out. *"Long night,"* he said, to nobody in particular.

*"Work night,"* Eli said.

*"Work night,"* Lena said.

The truck went on toward the stackyard. She stood in its dust and found that it was possible to be annoyed by her own words in another person's mouth.

She took the first photograph out of its sleeve and walked to the top of the house forty.

It had been off the water for eight days. On Friday that had not shown. It showed now. The green had gone gray underneath, like a shirt washed too often, and the leaves had folded themselves shut down the stems to keep what they had. The purple flowers were going brown. There was dust on all of it. Her own line was still faintly there, sixty-two steps of it.

She held the photograph up beside the field. One of them was the picture on Nora Brennan's refrigerator. The other one was what the photographer would be standing in at four.

A pickup had pulled up at the pole building, and a man in a cap was undoing the padlock. He was past forty and he had his mother's jaw.

*"You're the wedding,"* he said, when she walked over.

*"You're a son."*

*"Kenny. The other one's smarter."* He got the chain off and hung it on the door. Across the yard the mule had come to the rail of his pen to watch the padlock. *"What can I do for you?"*

*"Is that your pump down at the creek?"*

*"Mom's."*

*"Could it run on that field for a couple of hours? I've got a photographer coming."*

Kenny Hollenback looked at the house forty for a while. *"Eli's got that shut off."*

*"I know."*

*"He'll have it off for a reason. He's got a reason for how he parks."*

*"He's firming the ground so he can get a swather on it. He told me Saturday."*

*"Well, then."*

*"Two hours of water isn't going to turn it to mud."*

She heard herself say it. She had no idea whether it was true. She did not know what two hours of water did to anything except a centerpiece.

*"Won't green it up either,"* Kenny said. *"Not by four. And that's one line. It does eighty feet, right where it sits. It won't do forty acres."*

*"Where does it sit?"*

He pointed. Fifty yards down from the barn a long pipe on wheels lay clear across the field. She had taken it since Thursday for some kind of fence. It ran across the bottom of her sixty-two steps, through the place where the ground leveled.

*"Eighty feet is all a camera needs,"* she said. *"And it doesn't have to be green. It has to stand up and have the dust off. A camera can't tell wet from green."*

He thought about it, and he took as long as his mother would have. *"It's Mom's water,"* he said, *"and I hear you're Mom's roof. I've got to go to Livingston for a gearbox. I'll start it. You shut it off. There's a red handle on the pump, and down is off."*

*"Two hours."*

*"Two hours. And you tell him. I'm not in this."*

*"I'll tell him."*

She meant it. Eli was two fields away on top of a load of hay, and she was going to tell him the next time the truck came by.

The pump coughed twice down in the creek trees and caught. Out on the house forty the pipe shuddered from one end to the other. Every sprinkler along it ticked around once and threw. The water went up in the sun, forty feet to each side, and came down on the gray alfalfa with a sound like a hand smoothing a tablecloth. In about a minute there was a rainbow in it.

It was quarter past ten. She set an alarm on her phone for quarter past twelve.

The smell came up the slope to her, wet dust and then wet green. It was the prettiest thing she had made since she got to Montana. She stood and watched it, and her stomach was not happy about any part of it.

---

Bea's van came up the drive at half past ten. She carried the trial bouquet across the yard in a mason jar, in both hands, like something that might spill. It had garden roses in it, cream ones and a few the pink of the inside of a shell, and lavender standing up through them.

*"That's her,"* Bea said. *"That's the bride's. Tell me what the mother says about the lavender and I'll tell you what I'll say back. Keep it cool and keep it out of the sun."*

The coolest place on the property was the barn office. Lena put the jar on the desk beside the radio. Bea came as far as the door and took a good look at the whiteboard, with the gray cloud across the middle of it.

*"Mm-hm,"* she said.

*"Thank you, Bea."*

*"I didn't say anything."*

The flatbed went by twice more before eleven, loaded going one way and empty the other. Both times Lena was on the main floor with the tape, and both times she lifted her head, and the truck was already past. She could have walked out into the yard and put a hand up. He would have stopped. He would have looked down at her from the top of eight tiers of hay, with Dalton listening, and she would have had to say it up to him. *I turned your water on.* She told herself the third time would do.

Her phone rang at twenty to twelve. The barn had no bars, so she took it out to the yard and stood where there were two.

*"Lena,"* Corinne said.

Corinne had never needed more than that. She had a low voice and she did not hurry it, and for seven years the sound of it saying *Lena* down a corridor had meant that something was about to be either praised or taken away.

*"Corinne. Hi."*

*"I've had the rental people twice. They left a message with my service on Saturday to say they'd delivered you early, and they called my desk this morning with a driver's note. I'm going to read it to you. 'Venue has hay operation on site. Baler in yard. Tables stored in hay barn.'"* She let it sit. *"What is a hay operation doing in my venue."*

*My venue.* It was her letterhead, and it was her account.

*"It's a working ranch,"* Lena said. *"The owner leases her fields out. There was some equipment on the main floor when I got here Thursday, and it was off the floor by Saturday."*

*"And the tables are in a hay barn."*

*"They're in a bay off the main floor. It's swept. They're under cover and off the ground, the way your contract likes them."*

*"Is there hay in it?"*

*"No."*

That was true today. It would be true until some day after Wednesday.

*"So there is nothing I need to know."*

Behind Lena the sprinklers were going around, tick, tick, tick. Far off across the north meadow she could see the flatbed standing in the stubble, and one figure throwing bales up to another.

*"There's nothing you need to know."*

*"You'd tell me."*

*"I'd tell you."*

*"Good. Because Nora and I fly in Thursday, and I told that family you were the best I trained. I don't say it often. I've said it twice."*

*"Who was the other one?"*

*"She has her own firm in Dallas and she doesn't return my calls."* Corinne turned a page. *"Now. Tom's sister."*

Tom's sister could not be seated within sight of Tom's first wife. After Tom's sister there was an uncle who was vegan and a second uncle who would say something about it. Then came the question of whether a godmother gave a toast or a reading, and Corinne had a view. She went down the guest list the way she had always gone down one, without stopping, and Lena walked the yard with the phone at her ear and wrote on the back of her hand because the binder was in the barn.

The alarm went off against her cheek at quarter past twelve. She swiped it away in the middle of the vegan uncle. She was going to walk to the pump the moment Corinne drew breath.

Corinne drew breath at five to one.

---

The office door was propped open, as it always was. The back half of a gray mule was standing in it.

Lena stopped. The front half was at the desk. The mason jar lay on its side in a spread of water, and Deacon had the last of the roses in his mouth. He was chewing with his eyes half shut. He had eaten every rose in the bouquet down to the stem. The lavender lay on the desk beside the jar in a small tidy heap, not one sprig of it touched, like bones on the side of a plate.

*"That's Deacon,"* Eli said behind her.

He had come in from the yard for a ball of twine. He had chaff in his hair and his shirt was soaked through, and he was standing nearer to her than he had stood all morning.

*"I know who it is. He's eaten a hundred and eighty dollars' worth of garden roses."*

*"He left you the purple."*

*"That's lavender."*

*"He doesn't care for it."*

*"Nobody cares for it. It's in there for the mother of the bride."*

*"Then he's saved you an argument."*

The mule turned his head and regarded them both. He picked one sprig of lavender off the heap with his lips, thought about it, and let it fall on Eli's boot.

Lena laughed. She did not want to and she did it anyway, and when she looked up Eli had the line at the corner of his mouth, and for a second it was one in the morning again with the windows gone white.

*"About the work night,"* he said.

Then he turned his head toward the door.

She watched him listen. He stood the way he had sat in the cab, half turned, with his whole attention gone out of the room. It was very faint from in here.

Tick. Tick. Tick.

*"What's running,"* he said.

She looked at her phone. It was four minutes past one.

*"Lena. What's running."*

*"The line on the house forty. Since quarter past ten. It was meant to be two hours, I had an alarm set, I was on the phone with—"*

He was gone before she got to the end of it. He did not go toward the house forty. He went out the big door and south, running, and she did not know why south.

She ran for the creek and the red handle.

---

The wheel line on the south piece was up against the top fence. He had rolled it there on the tenth to get it out of the way of the swather. He had shut the whole system down at the pump on the eighth, and he had not gone back to close a valve on a dry pipe, and nobody would have.

Every head on it was throwing.

They threw forty feet into the field, and the breeze coming up the valley carried it twenty more. There were five windrows under the water, the five nearest the fence, the whole length of the piece. He had cut them on Saturday. They were two days down and nearly made, and he had meant to rake tonight and bale at one. The rest of the field lay pale and dry in the sun. Those five rows were dark green and flat, and they shone.

He was fifty yards off when the water quit. The heads sagged one after another down the line and dribbled, and then it was quiet enough to hear the pipe draining.

Eli knelt at the first row and pushed his hand in. It was wet clear through. It was cold, and it had the weight of a towel left in a sink, and at the bottom it had already begun to pack down onto the stubble.

He stood up and looked back at the forty. He could see the line on it from here, lying clear across the field below the barn, and the alfalfa standing up bright for forty feet on either side of it. That was two and a half acres of his best ground, and he had spent eight days drying it down. A swather would mark it now. Whatever he cut on that strip would lie on damp dirt and cure a day behind the rest of the field.

She was coming down across the stubble from the creek. She was wet to the knees and she had mud up one arm to the elbow, and she was not running anymore. She walked the last of it. She stopped at the end of the row he was standing in and looked at the hay, and he saw her understand what she was looking at.

*"I shut it off,"* she said.

*"I heard."*

*"I didn't know it ran down here."*

*"Did you know why it was off up there?"*

She could have taken a second over that. She did not. *"Yes."*

*"How."*

*"You told me. On Saturday, in the office."*

*"So it's not that you didn't know."*

*"No. I knew about the forty. I asked Kenny to start the pump, and he said you'd have a reason, and I told him what the reason was. And then I asked him again."*

*"For a picture."*

*"For the picture she's had on her refrigerator since February. Yes."*

He looked at her. She had her chin up, as she'd had it on Thursday when he said no, and her eyes were wet, and she was not doing anything about either one.

*"You measured my baler,"* he said. *"You know how wide that door is to the inch. You asked the electrician how many amps. And you never asked anybody where a pipe went."*

*"No."*

*"That's three ton of hay under there. I can bale around it tonight. Those five rows I have to turn, and turning knocks the leaf off, and the leaf's the feed. If I'm lucky it bleaches and I sell it to a man with cows for seventy dollars a ton less. That's two hundred dollars. If I'm not lucky it molds, and that's six-fifty. And you've put two and a half acres of soft ground across the middle of the forty. Whatever day I cut it, that strip lies there one day longer than the rest."*

*"I'll pay for the hay."*

*"With what?"*

*"With my own money. It wasn't the wedding's mistake."*

*"Then you'd better hope it bleaches."*

That landed. He saw it land, and he found he had wanted it to.

*"You've got forty thousand dollars lying on the ground,"* she said. Her voice had gone lower. *"I know that. You told all of Teagan's what forty minutes costs. I have one wedding. If it goes wrong, I don't lose three ton. I go home to Chicago and there isn't a second one."*

*"That's not the hay's fault."*

*"I never said it was the hay's fault. It's mine. I knew, and I did it, and then I let it run an hour over."*

*"On the phone."*

*"Yes."*

*"Who with?"*

*"It doesn't matter who with."*

It mattered. He could see that it did. He let it go, because he had hay to turn and he was too angry to be careful with her.

*"What does it do to Thursday,"* she said.

*"You're asking me about your aisle."*

*"I'm asking what I did. All of it. I want the number."*

*"The strip lies a day longer. Maybe more."*

*"The strip is where the bride stops."*

*"Yeah."*

*"So I've made it worse for both of us."*

*"Yeah."*

A magpie came down onto the wheel line and walked along the pipe. Neither of them looked at it.

*"You started to say something,"* she said. *"In the office. About the work night."*

*"It was a work night."*

*"You said that at ten this morning."*

*"So did you."*

*"Dalton was sitting right there."*

*"Dalton's not here now."*

He did not answer her. He could have. He had it ready, and he had been carrying it since six that morning, when she put on his gloves. He had carried it up and down the north meadow on top of four loads of hay, working out how a man said it. It would not come out past five wet windrows.

*"Go meet your photographer,"* he said.

*"Eli."*

*"I've got hay to turn."*

He walked up the field to get the tractor and the rake. He did not look back.

---

The photographer was a woman from Bozeman with two cameras and a stepladder. She walked out to the top of the house forty at four o'clock, stopped, and said a word she then apologized for.

It was standing, for eighty feet. Inside the strip every stem had come up off its neighbor and every leaf had opened, and the dust was gone. The sun was behind the creek trees. It came through the wet alfalfa from the back and lit it from the inside, and the drops were still on. Above the strip and below it the field was as gray as it had been at ten. The photographer looked at that for a moment. Then she got down on her knees in the wet, and from down there it was green all the way to the trees.

It was the picture on the refrigerator. Lena had made it so, and she stood in it with mud dried up to her elbow.

*"Where does she walk?"* the photographer said.

*"From the barn door. Sixty-two steps. She stops where the ground levels."*

The woman shot for forty minutes. She shot from the ladder and from her knees. She shot the barn through the alfalfa and the alfalfa through the barn door, and the main floor with nothing on it but fourteen circles of blue tape and the light coming down in stripes. Then she came back up the slope with the big camera turned around in her hands.

*"I'll send the bride ten tonight. I think this one's your cover."*

It was the aisle, low and long. The green ran down to the trees, and the mountains came up over the left side just where Lena had known they would. It was the photograph. Nora was going to cry at her refrigerator.

Lena looked at it for a long time. At the far left edge, past the last of the creek trees, there was a corner of another field, and in the corner there was a tractor pulling a rake. It was very small. She could not have said how she knew the man on it from the way he sat, at that size, but she did.

*"I can take the tractor out,"* the photographer said. *"It's two clicks."*

Lena went on looking at it.

*"Take it out,"* she said.
