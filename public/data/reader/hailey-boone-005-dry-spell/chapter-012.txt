It rained on Nora Brennan's wedding from ten past twelve on Saturday morning until sometime in the small hours of Sunday, and by noon Lena had stopped apologizing to anyone for it.

*"Day ten,"* she had told the binder at seven, and that was the last thing she said to it all day.

At four o'clock a hundred and forty people sat in two blocks of seventy on the main floor of the Hollenback barn, facing sixty tons of hay. The lights came up the wall from below. The rain was so loud on the tin that the string quartet had given up at ten to and put their bows in their laps. Nobody minded. The barn smelled like a field.

Lena stood inside the big door with a box of tissues.

*"You said handled,"* Colleen Brennan said. She was in blue, and she had started crying in the car. *"The very first day. You said I'd walk through this door and need one of those."*

*"I said it nine days too soon."*

*"Is it handled now?"*

*"No. It's done. That's different."* She held out the box. *"I said I'd be standing here."*

Colleen took three.

Tom's sign stood on an easel by the door, three feet wide and green to the trees, with no tractor in it. He had looked at it for a while that morning, and then at the wall of hay. *"There was a fellow on a tractor in the first one,"* he said. *"Nora's photographer showed me. I liked him."*

The dog lay across the aisle at about the twentieth step. She had picked the spot at noon and nobody had been able to shift her.

Nora came through the door on her father's good arm. She had her hair up and her grandmother's earrings on, and when she saw the wall she stopped. Tom stopped with her. A hundred and forty people turned around in their chairs.

*"It's greener than the picture,"* Nora said.

*"It's a storeroom,"* Corinne said from the second row, audibly. *"I've had it lit."*

They walked. It was forty-one steps indoors. Lena had counted. They went down between the dark bales of the strip, and at the twentieth they stepped over the dog, who lifted her head for the bride and put it down again for her father.

Lena did not watch the rest from the door. She watched it from the side, beside the office. Somebody was standing there in a clean shirt, bareheaded, and there was room next to him.

*"That's my hay,"* Eli said, under the rain.

*"That's my aisle."*

The groom's name was Owen, and he had to shout *I do* to be heard. So did she. Around the exchange of rings a gray mule walked in at the big door, dripping, and went down the aisle at the pace of a late guest. He stopped at the last bale on the left and began to eat it. The priest looked at Lena. Lena looked at Mrs. Hollenback. Mrs. Hollenback sat with her clipboard on her knee and her face lit up like a lamp, and she did nothing at all.

*"He doesn't care for lavender,"* Eli said. *"He'll eat an aisle."*

The bouquet had twice the lavender. Colleen had said at noon, through a tissue, that the lavender was her favorite part, and Bea had said she would let the mule know.

Nora was laughing when she was kissed.

They turned the room in ten minutes and fifty seconds. Corinne timed it on her phone. It was Lena and Dolores and Bea and Dalton, and Dalton's mother, who had not been asked. Corinne did the sixth place herself with her shoes off. When the last round was down and the cloths were straight she held the screen out for Lena to see, and put the phone away.

There was dancing, on the dance floor, where a swather had stood. The roof let go in one place about nine, over the bar wall, in a steady drip, and Mrs. Hollenback put a mason jar under it and stood looking up.

*"That's what you were for,"* she said. *"I told you. You're the roof."*

Wyatt Reeves left at ten with June asleep against the truck window. The hen was on his dashboard. She had walked the whole wet yard past every tire in it without a look, and gotten up into his cab on her own.

*"She's ready,"* Wyatt said. He nodded toward the barn. *"Tell him I said he did it right. He'll know which part."*

Late on, Corinne found Lena by the bar wall, beside the jar.

*"Come back,"* she said. She did not lead up to it. *"I don't mean as you were. Your name under mine on the door, and the whole Midwest book in three years, when I've had enough. I have never said that to anybody."*

Lena looked at the room. She looked at fourteen tables and a wall of hay, and at a tall man by the office door who was being talked at by the best man and was bearing it.

*"Can I answer you Monday?"*

*"You can answer when you like. I'd prefer a word."*

*"It won't be a word,"* Lena said.

---

The last shuttle left at half past twelve. By one there was nobody in the barn but the two of them and the dog, and the dog was asleep in the aisle.

*"I've never been up there,"* Lena said. She was looking at the loft.

*"It's quilts and bunting. Merrill keeps the dance things in it."*

*"Tom says it was higher."*

*"It's eleven feet. You measured it."*

She took off her watch. She laid it on the binder, on table one, and she left them both there. He had not seen her walk away from that binder before, with it in reach. She went up the ladder ahead of him in her good dress and bare feet.

It was dry in the loft, and close under the roof. The rain was a foot above their heads and it drowned everything. There was no light but what came up from the wall of hay, green and gold, through the rail. She found Merrill's quilts and threw down four of them.

*"There's no clock up here,"* she said.

*"No."*

*"No dew to catch. We've got all night. I've never had that with you."*

She undressed him. Her hands had half mended, enough for buttons, and she did every one. He stood and let her. Then she turned her back to him, and he found the zipper, and she stepped out of the dress and left it on the boards. She looked at him in that light for a good while, all of him, and put her hand around his cock and held it, not moving, watching it thicken in her fist.

She put him on his back on the quilts and got over him. It was hers. He saw that in the first minute, and he lay still and gave it to her. She took him into her slowly and sat up straight with her hands on her own thighs. She moved when she wanted and stopped when she wanted. Once she bent and kissed him for a long time without moving at all. He kept his hands on her hips and did not steer.

*"Talk to me,"* she said.

*"You're the best thing I ever had in that cab."*

*"Is that a long list?"*

*"It's a dog and a thermos."*

She laughed, and it tightened her around him, and he said her name through his teeth. She rode him harder then. She had her head back, with the green light on her throat. He put his thumb where she liked it and she came with a cry that the rain took. He had not heard her let go like that. In the cab there had been a field waiting, and in the bunkhouse the heat, and on Wednesday they had hardly made a sound.

She lay down on his chest until she had her breath.

*"Your turn,"* she said.

*"That's my line."*

*"I've booked it. Get up."*

She took him to the rail. They stood at it side by side with nothing on and looked down. Below them was the whole floor. There were fourteen tables with the cloths pulled crooked and the mason jars empty, and an aisle of dark bales, and thirteen feet of hay with the light going up it. A gray mule was asleep on his feet at the end of the aisle.

*"Look what you did,"* she said.

*"Look what you did."*

He turned her around. He put her back against the post at the end of the rail and lifted her, with his hands under her thighs, and she put her arms around his neck and her legs around his waist and held his eyes. He went into her like that, standing, face to face. He had not had the room for it in a cab.

*"I could stop right here,"* he said. *"Stay in you an hour and not move."*

*"Don't you dare. Fuck me, Eli. I want to watch you come."*

He kept on, long and deep, with the rain a foot over their heads. She watched him the whole time, as she had on Wednesday, and it was nothing like Wednesday. When he came he said her name with all the rest of it after, the things he had not said on a chair. She said *"there, there you are,"* very gently, as if she had been waiting for him somewhere.

They went back to the quilts. He did not know what time it was, and he found he liked that. Some while later he put his mouth on her and took as long as he wanted. Later again she woke him with her hand, and then with the rest of her. That time nobody was in charge of anything.

A drop of water landed between his shoulder blades.

*"Roof,"* he said.

*"I'm the roof."*

*"You're leaking."*

They moved the quilts three feet. It found them again inside ten minutes, on her ankle this time. He went down the ladder with nothing on and came back with a mason jar from the bar, and set it under the drip, and they lay and listened to it. It rang, and then it plinked, and then it had enough in it to go quiet.

*"What's that come to?"* she said. *"All of it. Since midnight Friday."*

*"I put the gauge out on the porch rail. I'll read it in the morning."*

*"You put it out."*

*"There was something to put in it."*

She was on her side, facing him. He could see her eyes. There was nothing going wrong anywhere on the place. The hay was dry and the bride was married and the machinery was around the back of the pole building. He had nothing in his hands.

*"Stay,"* he said.

She did not answer right away. She put her palm against his face.

*"That's how you ask,"* she said.

*"I know. I heard how I did it Friday."*

*"I'm going to answer you on Monday."*

*"Is it going to be a word?"*

She moved in against him. *"You'll get it in writing."*

---

The gauge on the porch rail said eight-tenths of an inch.

He showed it to her at seven on Sunday morning, holding it up to the light like a man with a good drink. The valley had gone a different color overnight. The paper-bag hills were dark, and steam was coming off the stubble of the forty.

Lena drove to Bridger Creek for a clean shirt and drove back.

Corinne flew at noon. So did the Brennans, all but the two who mattered, who had gone to Yellowstone. By two o'clock Lena was at the table in Mrs. Hollenback's kitchen with a piece of peach pie, a pen, and the binder open in front of her.

The binder had ten tabs. There was nothing behind the tenth but blank ruled pages. She took one out, and the rings snapped shut on the gap.

She wrote it by hand. It took her an hour and she wasted two sheets.

*VENUE AGREEMENT. The Hollenback barn and grounds, Saturday, August 19, 2028, with ten days before for setting. Booked by Lena Mercer, in her own name.* There was no letterhead. She did not own any yet. Under *Couple* she ruled a line and left it empty. She had no clients for it. She had a date and a barn, and she had never booked in that order in her life.

She did not write *exclusive use.* She wrote that the barn was rented subject to the hay lease attached, in every word of it, main floor, hay bay, and office, through the stacking of the last cutting. She wrote that the hay came first until it was in, and the wedding came first on the day. She wrote that if the two fell in the same week, the planner and the lessee would work it out between them, on the place, and that each would tell the other of any change to their face and before it was written anywhere.

*"That last part won't hold up,"* June McClaren said. She had come by for the pie, and read it standing, with her glasses on. *"You can't enforce 'to their face.'"*

*"I know. I want it in."*

*"Then it's a nice form."* June initialed the corner. *"That's eleven minutes. You owe me a slice."*

Mrs. Hollenback went to the drawer under the wall phone and took out the lease. She read Lena's page through twice, without her glasses, with her lips moving.

*"This one I'd be doing on purpose,"* she said.

*"Yes."*

*"Knowing."*

*"Both of us knowing. All three."*

*"Well, isn't that something."* She signed it with the same loop on the H. Then she turned to the last page of the lease, where there were twelve signatures of hers and twelve of his, one pair for every March, and she wrote the date for the thirteenth and signed that as well, seven months ahead of time. *"He can do his on the hood of the truck. I'm not waiting on a wet June again."*

Lena got the staple gun out of her bag. She put the lease behind her own page, squared the corners, and stapled them once, top left.

She took a picture of it with her phone. She sent it to Corinne with nothing written underneath.

It was nine at night in Chicago when the answer came.

*I can read. The one in Dallas doesn't return my calls. See that you do.*

---

On Monday morning there was a swather on the dance floor.

He had brought them all back in at seven, after the trucks came for the tables and the chairs. The swather was in the middle, with the tractor and baler behind it and the flatbed at the back. The wall of hay stood in the bay as he had always stacked it. The strip lay out along the floor in two rows with the air going around it, and one bale at the end had been eaten down to the strings.

The board in the office still had its one line on it. He had not been able to wipe it off, and he had not thought of anything good enough to put under it.

He was under the baler with the grease gun when he heard boots on the boards.

They were not new anymore. He knew the step. He pumped the fitting four times, because it needed four, and then he slid out and got a knee under him and stood up.

She had the binder on her hip. She had a white shirt on and her hair in its knot. She looked at him, and at the grease gun, and at the machine with the row of teeth standing where fourteen tables had been.

*"Hi,"* she said. *"Lena Mercer. I'm the planner for a wedding in this barn on the nineteenth of August next year. I have it for the ten days before."*

*"Eli Brandt."*

*"Are these yours."*

*"They are."*

*"Great. I'm going to need them moved."*

She waited for the rest of the sentence. She was not good at keeping her face straight and she was doing it by main force.

*"I've got a signed agreement,"* she said.

*"So do I."*

*"I know you do. It's stapled to mine."* She took it out of the third tab and put it in his hands. *"Hold this."*

He read it. He took his time, and this time it was the right way up. He read her own name, and the date. Under *Couple* there was a ruled line with nothing on it. He read *subject to the hay lease attached.* He read the part June had said would not hold up, twice. Then he turned to the back, where Merrill had signed for March with his own line left empty under hers.

*"That's thirteen,"* he said.

*"You said it would stop at twelve."*

*"I did say that."* He turned back to the front. *"Whose wedding is it?"*

*"I don't know yet. I haven't met them."*

*"You booked a barn for people you haven't met."*

*"I booked a barn and a hay man. I've got until Christmas to find two people who want to be married in front of sixty tons of hay. After Saturday I don't think it'll take that long."*

He looked at the empty line a moment longer than he needed to.

*"And if you can't find them?"*

*"It's a blank line, Eli. I'm thorough."* Her color had come up. *"I'm not that thorough."*

There was a second sheet under the staple, loose, eight inches by ten. It was the house forty, low and long and green to the creek trees. At the far left edge, past the last of them, was a tractor pulling a rake. It was very small. Anyone who knew him would have known how he sat.

*"I had you taken out,"* she said. *"On the Monday. It was two clicks. I've had you put back."*

He held that one by its edges.

*"I'm in Chicago till the end of September,"* she said. *"It takes four people most of a day to pack my apartment, and two of them carry binders. I'm back the first of October. I walk every venue twice, and I've only walked this one once."*

*"The bunkhouse is only mine till the thirty-first."*

*"Then you've got four weeks to think where a hay man winters."* She took the papers back and put them in the third tab. *"You didn't ask what happens if June's wet again."*

*"If it's a dry June I cut the house forty the last week of July, and I'm stacked and off your floor by the fifth of August. If it's a wet one I'm in your ten days again. It'll cost me stand and I'll cut it anyway. I'll know by the Fourth of July, and I'll call you that day."* He put the grease gun down on the frame of the baler. *"That's me telling you. It's not on anything."*

She did not say anything for a moment. Her eyes had gone bright.

He walked her out to the car. The dog was in the gravel, one space left of it. Lena put the binder on the passenger seat, and before the car could ask, he leaned in at the window and buckled it in.

*"Three hundred and sixty-two days,"* she told it.

*"Thirty-nine,"* he said. *"Till October."*

She got in. She started the engine and sat with both hands on the wheel, looking straight ahead at the cottonwoods, and then she turned and looked at him.

*"One thing I couldn't put in so it would hold,"* she said. *"Am I ever going to read it on a board again?"*

*"No,"* Eli said.

She kissed him through the window. Then she put the car in gear.

He stood in the yard until the dust had gone down the drive and the trees had closed over it. Clutch got up. She walked to the bare rectangle of gravel where the car had been and lay down in it with her chin on her paws, facing the road.

He sat down in the gravel beside her.
