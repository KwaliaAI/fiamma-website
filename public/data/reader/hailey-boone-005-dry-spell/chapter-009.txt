The SUV came up the cottonwood drive at twenty to six. Lena had been standing in the big door of the barn since five with the binder on her hip, and she had promised herself she would walk down and meet it, and she did not.

Nora Brennan got out of the driver's side. She was tall, with her hair in the knot of a woman coming off a twelve-hour shift, and she had a cardboard tube under one arm. Corinne got out of the other. She was sixty-one and she was wearing gray linen that had been on two planes and had not creased. She stood with one hand on the car door and looked at the barn from the roof down. She had looked at Lena's first table that way, seven years ago.

The hen came around the corner of the barn at her usual walk. She went to the SUV's front tire, studied it, and bit it.

*"Did that chicken just bite our car?"* Nora said.

*"Then you're staying,"* Mrs. Hollenback said. She had come down from the house with her clipboard and no plate. *"She doesn't bother with day visitors. I'm Merrill. Your father fell out of my loft in 1994."*

*"He says he proposed in it."*

*"He did both."*

Nora laughed, once. Then she saw Lena in the door, and it went.

She did not say hello. She walked across the yard and past the barn, around the north corner, with her phone already in her hand, and Lena put the binder down on a bale by the door and went after her. She was aware of a number of people. Bea was in the loft with her wire. Dolores was at the fly with six tasting plates laid out. Down at the stackyard the flatbed had come in with a load, and Dalton and the two Hollenback grandsons were sitting along the edge of it with a water jug, in plain view, forty yards off.

Nora stopped at the top of the house forty.

She held the phone up beside the field. Lena knew the gesture. She had made it herself, on Monday, with a photograph.

Forty acres of hay lay in rows on yellow stubble, all the way to the creek. He had raked it that afternoon. The rows were doubled now, fat and pale and exactly spaced. The mountains were where they had been. The creek trees still made a frame. At the far end a tractor was turning.

*"This was Monday,"* Nora said. She meant the phone.

*"Yes."*

*"It's Thursday."*

*"Yes."*

*"You said short. I've been telling myself short for eight hours. I thought, fine, mowed, like a lawn, Dad can still walk me."* She lowered the phone. *"This isn't short, Lena. This is gone."*

*"I know."*

*"Who are they?"*

*"He. It's one man. He leases the ground from Mrs. Hollenback and he grows hay on it. That's his crop. It was always his crop."*

*"Since when did you know that?"*

*"Friday."*

*"Which Friday?"*

*"The thirteenth."*

Nora was a nurse. She did not raise her voice. She went quiet and exact, as Lena supposed she did at a bedside when somebody had gotten a dose wrong.

*"I called you on Friday the thirteenth. I was at work. I said, okay, tell me the truth. That was the first thing I said to you. And you said I'm standing in it, it's up to my knees, the meadow's handled."*

*"I didn't know yet. I read the lease map four minutes after we hung up."*

*"And in the four minutes after that?"*

Lena had nine seconds ready. She had said them to a hen that morning. *It's a crop, it was never the venue's, I didn't tell you because I thought I could fix it.* They were for a woman who had not yet seen the field. Nora was standing in it. There was nothing left in them that she could not see for herself.

Lena had no answer that was any good, and she kept the bad ones to herself.

*"You had my number,"* Nora said. *"You've had it for six days. You sent me pictures. You had a woman kneel down so I wouldn't see the rest of it. That is what happened, isn't it? I looked at the cover on the plane and I thought, why is it so low."*

*"Yes. That's what happened."*

*"Dad's been showing that picture to people at the hardware store."*

She turned around and looked at the barn, at the high gray side of it with the west door standing open and the lights on along the loft. Her eyes were wet and she was furious about it. She had the tube against her chest in both arms. Lena had carried a bag of ice that way on Tuesday.

*"Is the inside real?"* she said. *"Or is that low too."*

*"The inside's real."*

*"I'd like to see something real."*

She went in by the west door. Lena stood in the stubble. It crunched. She had not walked on it until now.

---

Corinne had not come to the field. Corinne had gone into the barn.

She was on the main floor when Lena came in, standing very still at table one, with her fingertips on the cloth. Nora was walking between the rounds with her hand trailing over the chair backs at the head table. Nobody spoke. The room was doing what Lena had built it to do. The sun was through the gap in the siding and lay along the bar wall, and every glass on it was lit.

*"Oh,"* Nora said, to nobody. It was a small sound and it was the best thing that had happened to Lena in two days.

Nora had stopped at table one. She picked the card up off the cloth and read it. *Grandmother of the Bride.* She looked at the big door, and up at the loft, and across at the place by the bar wall where a cake table stood with nothing on it yet. She did it without moving her feet. Then she put the card back exactly where it had been, and she left her fingers on it.

Corinne took her fingers off the cloth. She walked to the edge of the bay, and looked into it, and stayed there.

*"Lena. Come here, please."*

Lena went.

*"What are these standing on."*

*"Pallets."*

*"Whose?"*

*"His."*

*"For what?"*

*"Hay."*

*"For the hay that is not in the bay."*

*"There wasn't any on Monday."*

*"No."* Corinne kept her eyes on the pallets. *"You were very careful on Monday. I went back over it on the plane. There was not one thing you said to me that wasn't true. I taught you to do that to caterers."*

She went from the bay to the office. Lena stayed where she was. She could see through the window onto the main floor. Corinne stood in front of the whiteboard with her reading glasses on and her arms folded, and she read it from the top, both colors, and it took her about a minute and a half. Lena watched her get to *DO NOT CUT.* She watched her get to *IT'S AN AISLE,* and *WED.,* and *I READ IT ON THE BOARD.* It was all up there. It was the whole eight days, in order, in two hands, and she had never once thought of it as something a person could walk in and read.

Corinne came out. She walked through the big door into the yard, where there was room, and where there were by now a good many people with reasons to be standing around. Lena went with her. It was a short walk.

*"That whiteboard,"* Corinne said, *"is the most informative document on this property. I've learned more from it in two minutes than from my own associate in a week. Does anybody here work for whoever writes in black?"*

At the stackyard a boy lifted his hand.

*"And you are?"*

*"Dalton, ma'am. I work for the black one."*

*"Then you know more about my event than I do. And it's my letterhead on the venue and my desk the rentals call."* She looked over at the fly, where Dolores stood with a serving spoon. *"Were you told anything?"*

*"Out by Thursday,"* Dolores said. *"The equipment. I wrote it down."*

*"In pencil?"*

*"That one's in pen."*

*"And you?"*

Bea had come to the top of the loft ladder, inside the big door. *"I wasn't told a thing,"* she said. *"I lost a bouquet to a mule and I've seen a whiteboard on the back of a shirt. I just do the flowers."*

Corinne took that in. It was to her credit that she left it alone. She turned to Lena. Her voice was as low as it had been in the bay. *"I asked you on Monday. I said, there's nothing I need to know. You said there was nothing. I said, you'd tell me. You said you'd tell me."*

*"Yes."*

*"Give me one reason you are not on the seven o'clock out of Bozeman."*

There was a way out of it. It was standing ten feet away in a gingham apron.

*"The barn was double-booked,"* Lena said. *"Before I ever got here. His lease gives him the main floor through the last cutting, and it's a good lease, a lawyer has read it. Mrs. Hollenback signed both. She knew on the Fourth of July that he'd be late and she never called anybody, she let me fly out here and find a baler on the dance floor, she knew for five weeks and she didn't—"*

She heard it then. She heard it the whole way through, each word, in her own voice, in front of Bea and Dolores and three boys on a flatbed.

Mrs. Hollenback had not moved. She held the clipboard flat against her chest.

*"I did know on the Fourth, and I should have telephoned, and I didn't, and that was wrong of me,"* she said. *"I'm sorry for it. What day did you know, honey?"*

She asked it as she had been asking people their names at a door for fifty years.

*"Thursday,"* Lena said.

*"Which Thursday?"* said Corinne.

*"The twelfth. About an hour after I got here. I read his lease at her kitchen table."*

Nora was in the big door behind them. Lena had felt her come. She turned around, because this part was not Corinne's.

*"The meadow I learned on Friday. That was true, what I said to you out there. The barn I knew on Thursday, an hour in. When your mother called and I told her it was handled, I had my back to his swather."*

Nora said nothing. She shifted the tube to her other arm.

*"That is a week,"* Corinne said.

*"Yes."*

*"In a week you told Colleen it was handled. You told Nora it was handled. And you told me there was nothing I needed to know."*

*"Yes."*

*"Why?"*

There were three true answers again, and this time she did not get to choose among them.

*"Because of what you told them about me. The best you trained. I thought if I fixed it before you saw it, that would still be so."*

Corinne took her glasses off. The anger had gone out of her face, and Lena would have given a good deal to have it back.

*"It was so,"* she said. *"That's what's stupid. Do you know why I said it? The cufflink. That child swallowed a cufflink at ten to four and you were in front of me by six minutes to, saying Corinne, the ring bearer has eaten a cufflink and I need the groom's brother's car. You didn't say handled. You said what it was. That was the planner I sent out here."*

Lena opened her mouth, and nothing she had was any use.

A man had come up from the field. She knew it before she looked. He had walked the whole length of the forty, and he was standing at the edge of the yard with his hat in his hand and chaff to the elbows. He was not breathing hard. He had been there long enough to hear most of it. Clutch got up from the barn door, went and stood beside him, and then came back and lay down again where she had been.

*"Ma'am,"* Eli said. *"It's my hay. I'm the one who cut it."*

*"And you are?"*

*"Eli Brandt. I lease the ground."*

*"The black one."*

His eyes went to Lena and came back. *"Yes, ma'am."*

*"Did you know there was a wedding?"*

*"Since the twelfth."*

*"And you cut it."*

*"Yes, ma'am. There's weather coming Saturday. It was Wednesday or lose the crop."*

*"Did you tell her you were going to?"*

It was very quiet in the yard. Somebody on the flatbed put the water jug down.

He could have said she knew. It was true. She had known, on his arm, with the radio on. He looked at Lena for a moment, and she saw him decide not to use it.

*"No, ma'am,"* he said. *"I said I'd tell her, and I didn't."*

It was the plainest thing she had ever heard him say.

*"Then that's between the two of you,"* Corinne said, *"and I'm afraid I don't care about it. My agreement is with Mrs. Hollenback. Lena. Inside."*

---

She took Lena to the far end of the main floor, under the loft, where the band would go. Bea had found something to do outside.

*"This is the best room you have ever set,"* Corinne said. *"I've seen every one of them. I want you to know I can see it."*

*"Thank you."*

*"Now listen. Tomorrow at six a hundred and forty people come up that drive for barbecue. By then I want somewhere for Nora to be married that isn't a hayfield and isn't a storeroom. I want every chair where a chair goes. I want no farm machinery in any sightline, and I want to walk through this building without reading about my own event on a wall. If I have that by six tomorrow, you run Saturday."*

*"And if you don't."*

*"I have four of my people holding seats on the six-ten on Saturday morning. They'll be here by ten. They are not as good as you. They'll put up a tent and it will be fine, and you'll go home."* She put her glasses in her bag. *"I'm not doing it to punish you. That's my goddaughter. I held her at the font."*

*"I understand."*

*"Don't tell me it's handled."*

*"I wasn't going to."*

*"Good."* Corinne looked at her a moment longer. *"You look terrible. Eat something."*

Nora was at the SUV with the door open. She had the tube in her hand still. When Lena came out she held it across the roof of the car.

*"It's Dad's sign,"* she said. *"Three feet wide. I don't know what you do with it now."*

Lena took it.

*"Is there going to be a meadow on Saturday?"*

*"No."*

*"Okay,"* Nora said. She got in. Through the window she added, not unkindly, *"That's the first thing you've told me I believe."*

The SUV went down between the cottonwoods. Dolores packed up six plates nobody had tasted. The flatbed emptied, and Dalton went home at six on the dot without looking at anyone, and Mrs. Hollenback walked back up to the house more slowly than she had come down.

---

He was in the office. She had known he would be.

He was standing in front of the board with the black marker in his hand, and the cap was on it. He turned around when she came in. She shut the door behind her. She had never seen that door shut. The prop had been in the floor so long that it left a mark.

*"You said it to her,"* Lena said.

*"She asked me."*

*"I asked you. On Tuesday. I asked you in your bed."*

*"I know."*

*"You had it. You had the whole sentence. I stood right here yesterday and waited for it, and you said my name, and I thought, he's going to write it under the chairs. And you had it the entire time, and you walked up a field and gave it to my boss."*

*"I had it yesterday. I couldn't see where on a board it went."*

*"It doesn't go on a board, Eli!"*

*"I know that. I knew it yesterday."* He put the marker down on the desk. *"I went to the motel. Tuesday, midnight. I sat across the lot from your door till your light went off."*

That stopped her. She had been awake behind that curtain. She had heard a truck.

*"Why didn't you knock?"*

*"Because you'd have asked me for a day. And I'd have given it to you."*

*"So you made sure I couldn't ask."*

*"Yes."*

*"You decided what I'd say, and then you decided I shouldn't get to say it."*

*"I decided I couldn't hear it and still cut."*

*"From where I was standing, holding a table, that is the same thing."*

*"I know it is."*

He was not defending himself. She would have liked him to. He stood there, and took it, and that left her with nothing in her hands but what she had done herself.

He gave her a moment, and then he gave it to her.

*"A week,"* he said. *"I heard her count it."*

*"Don't."*

*"You asked me on Tuesday if I'd tell you. Those were her words. I didn't know they were hers till I stood out there tonight."*

*"I said don't."*

*"You knew what you were asking me for. You knew what it was worth, because you'd given it to her on Monday. And you asked me anyway, and you lay there and waited to see if I'd do better than you had."*

She had.

*"I need the bay,"* she said. Her voice was not right. *"It's going to rain. It's the only dry room there is to marry her in. I have until six tomorrow."*

*"I need the bay. It's sixty ton. It's the only hay I put under a roof. I've got till it rains."*

*"I know."*

*"Then one of us is out."*

*"Yes."*

Neither of them said which. She looked at him. He had not slept. He had a split across one knuckle, and there was a place under his jaw where she had put a piece of ice two days ago and then her mouth.

*"I don't have anything else to say to you,"* she said.

*"No."*

*"I thought I would."*

*"So did I."*

She pulled the strip of gray linen out of her hair. She went past him to the board. She started at the top, at *NORTH,* where he had written it in again above the smear on Saturday, and she wiped. She took off his column and her column. She took off *DO NOT CUT* and *IT'S HAY* and *IT'S AN AISLE.* She took off Wednesday, and the chairs, and *IT WAS MY AISLE,* and the check mark she had drawn for Dalton, and the gray place the width of her shoulders that neither of them had ever written in. It all came off. It came off easily. Eight days went into a napkin runner.

He watched her do it, and said nothing about its being his schedule. He had it by heart, and so did she.

When she had finished, the board was white from edge to edge. The two markers lay in the tray, side by side, with their caps on.

She went out. She picked the binder up off the bale and the tube up off the gravel. The dog was in the middle of the big door, and Lena stepped over her, and the dog lifted her head and watched her go to the car and did not come.

She put the binder on the passenger seat. She did not buckle it in.

The car chimed for it the whole way to Bridger Creek, and she let it.
