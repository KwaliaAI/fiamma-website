At seven on Friday morning the swather was gone from the dance floor, and Lena gave herself the length of the barn door to enjoy it before she counted what was left. The tractor was left. The baler was left, and the flatbed, and far off to the north an engine was working at one steady pitch, like a lawnmower three yards over.

The dog was lying in the gravel exactly where Lena had parked yesterday. She had waited with her foot on the brake for the dog to move, and the dog had looked at the bumper and stayed, and in the end Lena had parked one space to the left.

*"Day two,"* she told the binder, and took it inside.

The hen was on the seat of the tractor. When Lena walked past with the tape measure she stood up, turned a full circle, and sat down again facing the other way.

She measured for an hour. The main floor was forty-eight feet by seventy-two, the loft rail was eleven feet up, and the door would take a fifty-six-seat shuttle's worth of guests four abreast. The hay bay opened off the far end with no wall between, forty-four feet across, and had a side door of its own onto the yard. The baler was nineteen feet four inches long. She wrote that down too. She wrote everything down.

The electrician came from Livingston at eight in a van with a ladder on it. He looked at the outlet in the office, and then he walked around to the panel on the outside wall of the pole building and looked at that, and then he came back.

*"You've got sixty amps out here,"* he said. *"That'll run his radio and hurt its feelings. What are you putting on it."*

*"A six-piece band, a caterer with two warming ovens, and four hundred feet of string lights."*

*"You want a generator."*

*"How big a generator."*

*"For that? Big."* He wrote a number on the back of a card and gave it to her. It was eleven hundred dollars for the weekend, delivered. Her contingency line was fifteen hundred, and it was the second day.

She called the generator company from the yard, because the yard had two bars and the barn had none. They could do the twentieth. They wanted half down. She read them the card number off the back of the binder and watched the contingency line go from fifteen hundred to four hundred, with eight days still to run.

After that she stood in the office doorway and looked at the whiteboard. The black capitals were where they had been. So was the red marker.

She took its cap off. It was stiff. She wrote on the right-hand side of the board, level with his first line, in letters the same height as his.

*SAT 14. 10 AM. VENDOR WALK-THROUGH. MAIN FLOOR CLEAR.*

*FRI 20 TO SUN 22. WEDDING. BARN EMPTY.*

Her capitals were neater than his, and she let herself have that.

*"Oh, good, you found the board,"* Mrs. Hollenback said behind her. She had the clipboard, and a paper plate with foil over it. *"June McClaren's eating at Teagan's at noon. She's a lawyer, a real one, she's licensed here now. She says bring both papers and she'll look. I told Eli."*

*"Will he come?"*

*"He'll come. He won't like it."* She put the plate on the desk. *"That's for whoever's here at three. Shut it in the drawer if you go out."*

*"Why?"*

Mrs. Hollenback pointed with the clipboard. Across the yard, in the pen beside the pole building, a gray mule had the gate chain in his lips and was working it along the post an inch at a time, patiently, with his eyes half shut.

*"That's Deacon. I've had him thirty years, and there's not a latch on this place he can't open. The boys keep a padlock on the pole building on his account."* She watched him a moment with some pride. *"He likes cornbread."*

---

Teagan's was on Main Street across from the bank. It had three booths, five stools, and a pie case, and at noon on a Friday two of the booths were full of men in caps who watched Lena come in and then went back to their plates.

A woman in the back booth lifted two fingers. She was about Lena's age, in a sleeveless shirt with reading glasses pushed up into her hair, and she had a legal pad squared in front of her with a pen laid across it. The man beside her was eating a chicken-fried steak.

*"June McClaren,"* the woman said. *"That's Wyatt. He's mine. He's eating."*

Wyatt raised his fork an inch.

Lena slid in across from them and put the binder on the table. The waitress set down a coffee she had not ordered and went away without asking her anything.

*"I hear you met my chicken,"* June said.

*"She bit my rental car."*

*"Front left?"*

*"Yes."*

*"She did that to mine. A year ago April."* June turned the legal pad a quarter inch. *"I live here now."*

Wyatt cut another piece of steak.

The door opened at six past twelve. Lena did not turn around. She knew who it was by the men in the caps, who all said *Eli* one after another in the same tone, and by the smell that came down the aisle ahead of him, cut grass and diesel. He stopped at the end of the booth. There was one place left to sit, and it was next to her.

He sat. The bench was built for two smaller people. His thigh came to rest along the whole length of hers, and he did not move it, and there was nowhere for her to move hers. He had green on him. There were bits of leaf in the hair on his forearm and a fine pale dust on his neck, and where his leg lay against hers she could feel the heat of seven hours in a glass cab coming through two layers of denim.

*"I left a swather idling,"* he said to June.

*"Then I'll read fast."*

June put her glasses on. Lena gave her the venue agreement out of the third tab and her phone with the four pictures of the lease, and June read both of them through once in silence, one finger moving, while Wyatt finished his potatoes. Then she read them out loud in a voice with no opinion in it at all.

*"'Exclusive use of the barn and grounds, August twelfth through August twenty-second, 2027.' Who drafted this."*

*"Corinne's lawyer. My old firm. It's the standard venue form."*

*"It's a nice form."* She picked up the phone. *"'The lessee shall have the use of the barn, including the main floor, the hay bay, and the office, for the storage of equipment and crop from first cutting through the stacking of the last.' Who drafted this."*

*"Merrill's lawyer in Livingston,"* Eli said. *"In 1988. For the man before me."*

*"It's a nice lease."*

*"So which one wins,"* Lena said.

*"Neither. They're both good."* June took her glasses off. *"Merrill had the right to grant either of these. She granted both. Yours doesn't cancel his because his is older, and his doesn't cancel yours because yours says exclusive. Each of you has a claim against Merrill for whatever the other one costs you. Neither of you has a thing against the other."*

*"I'm not suing Merrill,"* Eli said.

*"Nobody is suing Merrill,"* Lena said.

*"Then you have two good contracts and one barn."*

*"Salt,"* Wyatt said, and June passed it.

Lena put her finger on the first line of her own page. *"It says exclusive. Exclusive means nobody else."*

*"To a lawyer it means nobody else as against the person who promised it. Merrill can't put a third party in that barn."*

*"He's a second party."*

*"He was there first. Technically that makes you the second party."*

*"Twelve Marches,"* Eli said, into his coffee.

*"Do not."*

June looked at him. *"What does it mean to you?"*

*"Exclusive?"*

*"Yeah."*

He thought about it. *"Means nobody else is haying it."*

*"Nobody else is haying it,"* Lena said.

*"Then we're fine."*

*"And to a planner,"* June said.

*"It means empty. It means I walk in and there's a floor."*

*"There's a floor,"* Eli said.

*"There's a baler on it."*

*"Floor's still there. It's under the baler."*

He had turned his head to say it, and that put his face about ten inches from hers, and there was a line at the corner of his mouth that had not been there in the barn. He was enjoying this. She had the plain thought that she would like to see what else did that to his mouth, and she picked up her coffee and drank some of it, and it was terrible.

*"Wyatt made that,"* June said. *"Teagan lets him when it's slow. I'm sorry."*

The waitress came by with a plate for June and a plate nobody had ordered for Eli. June lifted the slice of tomato off her burger and laid it on the rim of Wyatt's plate without looking at it.

*"Store-bought,"* Wyatt said.

*"Not a subject."*

Eli ate half a sandwich in four bites. Lena watched his hands do it. She was aware of every inch of the leg beside hers and of the forty tables arriving Monday and of nothing in between.

*"So what do we do,"* she said.

*"What do you each need,"* June said. *"Leave what the paper says. What do you need."*

*"The main floor empty from Wednesday night. The meadow by the barn. Power for a band."*

June looked at Eli.

*"The door,"* he said. *"The hay bay. A week of this weather."*

*"Those aren't the same list."*

*"No."*

*"They aren't opposite lists either,"* June said. *"I'd have killed for two lists like that in Seattle. I once spent nine months on a driveway."*

*"And legally,"* Lena said.

*"Legally?"* June capped her pen. *"You work it out, or one of you sues Merrill, and a judge in Livingston gets to it around October. That's eleven minutes. You each owe me a slice of pie."*

Wyatt wiped his mouth and laid the napkin on his plate. He looked at Eli and then at Lena, and at the four inches of bench that neither of them was using.

*"You want my opinion,"* he said.

*"They don't,"* June said.

*"It's a big barn."*

*"People keep telling me that,"* Lena said.

Eli stood. He put a ten on the table and looked at the clock over the pie case. *"Forty minutes. That's three acres."*

*"You're billing me for lunch."*

*"I'm telling you what it cost."*

He went out, and the caps said *Eli* again, one after another. The whole left side of Lena's leg was cold. She put it down to the air conditioning, and the air conditioning in Teagan's was a fan.

---

Her site map called it the Ceremony Meadow. It ran from the west door of the barn down to the line of trees at the creek, and at three in the afternoon she stood at the top of it with the binder against her chest and felt, for the first time since Bozeman, that something in Montana was on her side.

It was green. The hills behind it were toast and the yard was dust, and this one long field was green to her knees and thick, with small purple flowers all through it and bees working them. It moved when the air moved. It was the photograph, and the photograph did not smell like this.

Her phone rang. NORA.

*"Okay, tell me the truth,"* Nora said. She was thirty and a pediatric nurse, and she said most things like a person with a chart in her other hand. *"Mom says you said it's better than the pictures. A girl at work pulled up Montana in August and it looks like toast."*

*"The hills are toast."*

*"Lena."*

*"The hills are toast and the meadow is green. I'm standing in it. It's up to my knees."*

*"The one in the picture? The one Dad walks me down?"*

*"That one."*

Nora let out a breath that Lena could hear in Chicago. *"Okay. Because the photographer's coming to you Monday for the venue shots, for the programs and the welcome sign, and I don't care about the hills. I care about that field. I've had that picture on my refrigerator since February. I need that exact green."*

A careful planner would have said *let me walk it and call you back.* Lena walked every venue twice. She had not walked this one once, and there was a lease in her phone with a map on the fourth page that she had photographed yesterday and had not read.

*"You'll have it,"* she said. *"Monday for the photographs and Saturday for the aisle. The meadow's handled."*

*"You're the best. Corinne said."*

*"She trained me."*

She put the phone in her back pocket and walked the aisle. She did it right, heel to toe from the barn door, counting, with the grass dragging at her knees and the bees getting out of her way. It was sixty-two steps to the place where the ground leveled and the creek trees made a frame. An arbor there, chairs in two blocks of seventy, the mountains over the bride's left shoulder. She could see all of it.

She got the phone out again to check where *barn and grounds* stopped, for the parking. She opened the fourth picture.

It was a map drawn by hand, in ballpoint, with the creek along the bottom and the barn as a small square with an X through it. There were three shapes with acreages written inside them. One said NORTH, 45. One said SOUTH PIECE, 35. The third was a long rectangle running from the X to the creek, and it said HOUSE 40.

Lena looked at the map. She looked at the barn behind her, and at the creek in front of her, and down at her own knees.

*HOUSE 40. ?*

She was sixty-two steps into forty acres of Eli Brandt's hay. She had walked a straight line through it and laid it over, and when she turned around she could see the line. She picked one of the purple flowers and looked at it up close. It was a crop.

She got as far as Nora's name on the phone. Her thumb was over it. *I spoke too soon, the field belongs to the hay man, give me a day.* It was one sentence and it was true, and Nora was a nurse and had heard worse before lunch.

But Nora would tell Colleen, and Colleen would tell Corinne by dinner, and what Corinne would hear was that Lena Mercer had promised a bride a meadow on her second day without finding out whose it was. Lena could fix this by Monday. She did not know how yet, but she had fixed a cufflink.

She put the phone away. She walked back up to the barn beside her own line, in the dust at the edge, and did not step on any more of it.

To the north the engine stopped, and started again, and went on.

---

Eli laid the last windrow of the north meadow down at ten to seven and shut the swather off at the gate.

He sat in the cab a minute with the door open. Forty-five acres lay behind him in long green rows, straight enough, and the smell came up off them into the cab, sweet and heavy, with a day's sun in it. Three days down if this held. He looked at the west out of habit and the west had nothing to tell him.

The passenger side of the cab floor was empty. Clutch had ridden there every cutting for six years. This morning she had walked with him as far as the cab steps, looked at them, and gone back to lie down in the gravel by the barn door, and he had stood there at five in the morning holding the door open for a dog.

He had also spent forty minutes of a dry day in a booth at Teagan's, and three of the forty-five acres had waited on it. He knew what the forty minutes had cost. He could not have said the same for what he had gotten out of it, apart from two good contracts and one barn, and the way she said *do not.* He had been thirty-six years old on that bench with his leg against a woman's, and neither one of them had moved for a whole sandwich.

*"That's enough of that,"* he said, to the windshield.

The house forty was the piece he thought about on the drive in. He thought about it most evenings. It was the oldest stand on the place and the best, four parts alfalfa to one of orchard grass, on ground that held water like a sponge, and it went in the barn every year and nowhere else. The north and the south he stacked outside under tarps and sold by the ton to people with cows. The house forty he sold by the bale, in February, to people with horses, at a price he did not say out loud at Teagan's. A third of the acres made half the money. He had put a question mark by it on Sunday because it was the one piece he could not afford to guess at.

Dalton had left the grease gun on the baler tongue and a note under it that said *did the rake, left side tire is low.* The kid wrote everything down, and Eli had not until this week known anybody else who did.

Her car was gone when he walked up to the barn. Clutch was in the gravel, one space over from where the rental had been. On the main floor there were long strips of blue tape stuck down in the space the swather had left, laid out in circles, fourteen of them. He walked around the outside of them without deciding to.

The office light was on. There was a paper plate on the desk with the foil turned back and one square of cornbread left on it, and there was red on his board.

He read it standing up.

*SAT 14. 10 AM. VENDOR WALK-THROUGH. MAIN FLOOR CLEAR.*

*FRI 20 TO SUN 22. WEDDING. BARN EMPTY.*

Those two were in a steady hand. The third had been written faster, lower down, across from his own third line.

*HOUSE 40 = CEREMONY MEADOW. PHOTOS MON 16. AISLE SAT 21. DO NOT CUT.*

She had also gone over his question mark in red, so that it stood there in both colors.

He went out and walked down to the top of the house forty in the last of the light. He found the line right off. Sixty-some paces of it, one woman wide, from the barn door to where the ground leveled, with the alfalfa pressed over to both sides. It would stand back up by morning, most of it. At the bottom there was a square trampled flat where she had stood and turned around a few times, looking at things.

So that was the ceremony. He had forty acres of the best hay he grew, six days from ready, and she had forty acres of aisle. Saturday the twenty-first, four o'clock, a hundred and forty people in good shoes. He stood in her square and looked where she had looked. The creek trees made a kind of frame from there, with the mountains coming up over the left side of it. He had cut this field for twelve years and had never once stood still in the middle of it.

She had written it on the board, though. She could have said nothing till Monday and let him find a photographer in it. She had come in off this field and put it up in red where he would read it the same night, and he turned that over on the walk back and could not make it come out to her advantage.

He took the black marker out of the tray. Under her first line he wrote, *CLEAR OF WHAT.* Under her second he wrote, *NO.* He stood in front of the third with the marker an inch off the board, and then he wrote what was true.

*IT'S HAY.*

He ate the cornbread. He went out to fuel the swather for the morning, and that took fifteen minutes, with the hand pump on the tank behind the pole building. Headlights came up the drive while he was at it and went down again. He did not walk around the barn to look.

When he came back in, there was red under his black. One line.

*IT'S AN AISLE.*

The marker on the tray was still uncapped, and the cap was on the desk beside the empty plate. He put it back on for her.
