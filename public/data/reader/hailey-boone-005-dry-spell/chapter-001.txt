The wedding binder weighed eleven pounds, and the rental car had decided at the Bozeman airport that anything weighing eleven pounds on the passenger seat was a person who ought to be wearing a seatbelt. It chimed about it. Lena Mercer told the car she was not going to buckle in a binder, held to that through four minutes of chiming, and then pulled over on the airport road and buckled in the binder.

It rode beside her now with the belt across its tabs. There were ten tabs, one for each day, Thursday the twelfth of August through Saturday the twenty-first, and behind the tenth was a wedding for a hundred and forty people in a barn she had only seen in photographs.

*"Ten days,"* she told it.

Her phone was in the cup holder with the forecast open. It showed ninety-one degrees in Bridger Creek and ten small suns in a row, the last of them sitting over Saturday the twenty-first. She had taken a screenshot of the suns at the gate in Chicago in case they changed their minds.

She had planned eleven weddings since she left Corinne's firm in March, and the biggest of them had sat sixty. The Brennan wedding was older than that. Lena had signed the venue in February, at her desk at Corinne's, on Corinne's letterhead, as the associate on the file. When she left, Corinne let the file leave with her. Corinne was the bride's godmother, and a godmother could not run a wedding she was supposed to cry at, so she had put Lena's name in front of the Brennan family with four words attached. *The best I trained.* Lena had the four words by heart. She also had the arithmetic on what happened to a planner five months out on her own whose first big job went wrong in front of the woman who knew every venue in Chicago.

There had been a site visit in Corinne's budget for May. The budget had stayed at Corinne's. Lena could afford one flight to Montana this year and she was on the far end of it, and so a planner who walked every venue twice was driving toward a barn she knew from eight photographs and a phone call.

She understood there was a river. She was not looking at it. She was looking at the road as it would come through the windshield of a fifty-six-seat shuttle bus, and counting the places a bus could not turn around. She had four of them when the hills got her attention anyway. They were the color of a paper bag, on both sides, all the way up.

She broke the promise she had made on the plane, the one where the binder stayed shut until she was standing in the barn, and flipped it open one-handed at the first tab. The photographs were there in their sleeves, the barn and the meadow and the hills behind, and every inch of it was green.

The photographs had been taken in June by one of the Hollenback grandsons. It said so in the corner of each one.

*"Okay,"* she said. *"Brown."*

She added it to Day One in her head, under *confirm power* and above *meet owner,* and noticed that Day One was now three items longer than the page she had printed for it.

Nora Brennan had never lived in Montana. Her father had proposed to her mother in the loft of the Hollenback barn, at a Fourth of July dance in 1994, and the family had told it every Thanksgiving since with the loft a little higher each year. Nora wanted the barn. Her mother wanted the loft. Lena had a deposit, a signed venue agreement, and a line in her budget for a tissue box at every pew.

*"Your destination is on the left,"* the phone said, at a mailbox.

The drive ran half a mile between cottonwoods, one vehicle wide, and she put *shuttle: one way, needs a flagger* on Day One before she was out of the trees. It came out in a yard with a ranch house on the right and the barn on the left, and a long metal pole building out behind. The barn was tall and gray and had a sliding door the size of a garage rolled all the way open. She parked in its shade and got out. Her boots were new. She had bought them for this job and broken them in on a hardwood floor, and the gravel was hot straight through them.

A hen came around the corner of the barn.

She came at a walk, in no hurry, like someone crossing a lobby to speak to the desk. She stopped at the rental's front tire, studied it, and bit it. She held on for a moment, let go, and stood back to see how the tire had taken it.

*"Hey,"* Lena said.

The hen looked at her and bit the tire again.

Lena had handled a drunk best man, a caterer who walked out at the salad course, and a ring bearer who swallowed a cufflink. She had no page for this. She got the binder out of its seatbelt, put it on her hip, and went around the chicken.

The barn was better than the photographs. It was ten degrees cooler inside and it smelled of dry grass and old wood, and the light came down through the gaps in the siding in long stripes. She had it counted before she had finished walking in. Fourteen rounds of ten on the main floor with a dance floor in the middle, the band at the far end under the loft, the bar on the north wall where the light would be kind at six o'clock.

Then she counted what was standing on the main floor.

In the middle, where the dance floor went, was a machine with a glass cab on top and a row of steel teeth across the front as wide as a two-car garage. Behind it stood a tractor, also with a cab, hitched to a long red machine with a chute at the back. Behind that was a flatbed truck, and in the dead center of the flatbed lay a border collie the color of a brick. The dog watched her come in without lifting its chin off its paws.

A pair of boots stuck out from under the red machine.

*"Hello?"* Lena said.

The boots did not hurry. There was a clicking sound, four or five times, something being pumped, and then a man slid out on his back, got a knee under him, and stood up. He kept standing up for a while. He was six-two or near it and built by work, somewhere around thirty-five, in a gray t-shirt gone dark down the spine, with a grease gun in one hand and both arms black to the elbow. He looked at her. He looked at the binder, and then at the boots, and he stayed on the boots a second longer than she liked.

She said, *"Hi. Lena Mercer. I'm the planner for the Brennan wedding, I have the barn from today."*

He said, *"Eli Brandt."*

*"Are these yours."*

*"Yeah."*

*"Great. I'm going to need them moved."*

*"No."*

She waited for the rest of the sentence. He wiped his free hand down the leg of his jeans.

*"No as in not today,"* she said.

*"No as in I'm haying. Second cutting starts in the morning."*

*"And when does it finish."*

*"When it's dry."*

*"Is there a date for that."*

*"There's weather for it."*

Lena had a voice for vendors who said no. It was pleasant and it never went up at the end of a sentence, and she used it now. *"Mr. Brandt, I have a signed agreement with Mrs. Hollenback for this barn."*

*"So do I."*

*"For these ten days."*

*"For the hay. The hay's these ten days."* He nodded past her at the open door. *"She's up at the house. She'll give you pie."*

*"I don't want pie."*

*"She won't ask."*

He got back down on the floor and slid under the machine, and the clicking started again. Lena stood where she was. She was thirty-one years old and nobody had ever ended a meeting with her by lying down. On the flatbed the dog had not moved, and gave the impression of having won.

---

Merrill Hollenback had the screen door open before Lena reached the porch steps. She was seventy-three, in a gingham apron, and she held the door with her hip and a clipboard against her chest. She had been checking people in at doors since before Lena was born and it showed in how she stood.

*"You're the wedding,"* she said. *"Sit down, there's pie."*

The kitchen had a table for eight and a fan in the window turning the heat over. The pie was peach. It was in front of Lena with a fork beside it before she had the binder flat on the table.

*"Mrs. Hollenback, there's a man in the barn."*

*"That's Eli."*

*"He has machinery in the barn."*

*"He has a swather, a tractor, a baler, and a truck in the barn,"* Mrs. Hollenback said, with satisfaction. *"He does every summer. He's the best hay man in this valley."*

*"He says he has a contract."*

*"Well, he does."* She went to the drawer under the wall phone, took out a stack of papers held with a rubber band, and found the one she wanted without her glasses. She laid it beside the pie. *"There. I did yours in February and his in March, same as every March."*

Lena read it. It ran four pages and it was a real lease. Irrigated hay ground in three meadows, a term from the first of April to the last of October, rent due in two halves. On page three, initialed by both of them in blue ink, the lessee had the use of the barn, *including the main floor, the hay bay, and the office, for the storage of equipment and crop from first cutting through the stacking of the last.*

She turned to the third tab in the binder and set her own agreement next to it. *Exclusive use of the barn and grounds, August 12 through August 22, 2027.* Corinne's letterhead ran across the top and Lena's own signature sat at the bottom, as associate. Beside hers was the same signature that was on the lease, with the same loop on the H.

*"Mine says exclusive use,"* Lena said.

*"It does. I liked that. It sounded like a hotel."*

*"His says the main floor."*

*"His always says that."*

*"Mrs. Hollenback. They can't both be true."*

*"Honey, they've both got my name on them. Any other year you'd never have known. Any other year he's stacked and gone by the first week of August, and you'd have had that barn to yourself and never laid eyes on him."* She sounded sorry about that part. *"Then it rained all June."*

*"When did you know he'd be late."*

*"Fourth of July or so."*

*"That was five weeks ago."*

*"I meant to call you. Then I thought, she'll see it when she gets here, and here you are."* She sat down across the table and looked at the two contracts side by side with the face of a woman whose favorite grandchildren have finally been put in the same room. *"It's a big barn. And he's quiet, and you look like you talk. I think it'll be nice for him."*

Lena ate a bite of pie because her mouth needed something to do. It was very good pie, and that did not help.

*"Can I ask why he's the one cutting your hay? I was told you had sons."*

*"I have two. They run cows. They'd sooner buy hay than make it, and Eli would sooner make it than talk about it, so everybody's suited."* Mrs. Hollenback tapped the venue agreement with one finger. *"And that barn wants a roof before the snow. My boys think a roof is something that happens to other people. So you're the roof."*

*"I'm the roof."*

*"You and a hundred and forty of your friends."*

Lena looked out the window over the sink. She could see the rental car in the shade of the barn, and the hen, who had finished with the tire and was sitting on the hood.

*"Is that your chicken."*

*"That's June McClaren's. She rides over on Eli's flatbed when he delivers to their place, and she goes home when she's ready. Her name is Garbage. I didn't name her."*

*"She bit my car."*

*"Did she."* Mrs. Hollenback looked pleased for the third time in ten minutes. *"Then you're staying. She doesn't bother with day visitors."*

Lena took a picture of each page of the lease with her phone. Then she finished the pie, since leaving it on the plate was not going to move a baler.

---

He was out from under the baler when she came back. He was standing at the teeth of the big machine with a wrench, and the dog was where she had left it.

She went to the office first. It was a room boxed into the near corner of the barn with a window onto the main floor, and Day One in the binder called it *vendor check-in.* She looked in. There was a desk, a coffee can of bolts, a cot folded against the wall, a radio plugged into the only outlet she could see, and a whiteboard as wide as the desk with black capitals down it.

*NORTH. Cut Fri 13. Bale Mon.*

*SOUTH PIECE. Cut Sat.*

*HOUSE 40. ?*

*ALL UP BY 23RD.*

None of those names was on her site map, and every one of those dates was in her binder. In the tray under the board lay a black marker worn gray at the grip, and next to it a red one that had never had its cap off.

*"Where's the panel,"* she said, through the door.

*"The what."*

*"The electrical panel. I have a band, a caterer, and four hundred feet of lights."*

*"There's an outlet."*

*"I saw the outlet. The radio's in it."*

*"That's the one."*

She wrote *POWER* on the Day One page and underlined it twice, and then she walked across the floor to him.

*"I've read your lease."*

*"Okay."*

*"It's a good lease."*

*"I know."*

*"Mine's good too. Hold this."*

She put the binder into his hands because she needed both of hers for the binder's rings, and he took it, and his arms did not dip. He held it flat like a tray while she opened it across his forearms and turned to the third tab. It put her about a foot closer to him than she stood to vendors. He smelled of grease and cut grass and a long morning. A line of sweat ran down the side of his neck into his collar, and she followed it all the way down, and she lost count. She could not have said of what. She found her place on the page a line too low and had to come back up for it.

She read him the date. She had known the date since February.

*"February ninth,"* she said. *"Signed and countersigned."*

*"March."*

*"February comes before March."*

*"Which March."*

*"This one."*

*"I've signed that lease every March since 2016."* He looked down at the page between them and took his time reading it, upside down. *"That's twelve Marches. You've got one February."*

*"That is not how dates work."*

*"It's how she works."* He tipped his head toward the house. *"What's in this thing."*

*"A wedding."*

*"Feels like more than one."*

*"It's a hundred and forty people."*

*"That'd do it."*

He had not handed it back. She had not asked for it. She was looking at his hands on the cover, and at the black grease in the creases of his knuckles an inch from her tab dividers, and she was still thinking about that when her phone rang in her back pocket.

COLLEEN BRENNAN, the screen said. The mother of the bride.

She should have let it ring and called back from the car, and she knew it. She took the binder out of his hands, walked twelve steps to the big door, and answered it there, in a barn that would carry a whisper from one end to the other.

*"Colleen. Hi. I just got here."*

*"Are you standing in it? Tell me. Is it like the pictures?"*

Lena looked out at the paper-bag hills. *"It's better than the pictures."*

*"Oh, I knew it. Tom wants to know if the loft is still there."*

She had not checked. She turned around, and it was, with a ladder going up to it, directly above a flatbed truck. *"The loft is still there."*

*"And they've got it all cleared out for you? Corinne said these working barns can be full of God knows what."*

Behind her the wrench had stopped.

*"The barn's handled, Colleen."*

*"Handled."*

*"Handled. Saturday the twenty-first at four o'clock you're going to walk through this door and need a tissue, and I'm going to be standing right here holding one."*

Colleen laughed, and said she was going to cry now, actually, and that Nora would call tomorrow about the photographs. Lena said that was wonderful. She hung up and stood with the phone pressed flat against her breastbone.

Something leaned on her shin. The dog had come down off the flatbed and crossed the whole floor without a sound, and it was sitting on her left boot with its shoulder against her leg, looking out the door at the same hills.

*"Clutch,"* the man said.

The dog did not turn its head.

*"The twenty-first,"* he said.

*"That was a private call."*

*"It was in the barn."*

*"It's handled,"* Lena said. *"It's a word. It means it's going to be."*

*"Huh."* He set the wrench down on the frame of the machine. *"I'll try it on the north meadow."*

She did not laugh, and it took some doing.

*"I'll be here at seven tomorrow,"* she said, *"with a tape measure and an electrician."*

*"I'll be here at five with a swather."*

*"Which one is the swather."*

He put his hand flat on the machine beside him.

*"That's the thing on my dance floor."*

*"That's the thing in the barn."*

She got her foot out from under the dog, which took negotiation, and walked out into ninety-one degrees with the binder on her hip. The hen had gone from the hood. There were two small marks in the dust on the front tire, side by side.

She put the binder on the passenger seat and buckled it in before the car could ask.

---

Eli Brandt changed nine sickle sections on the swather that afternoon, and it took him about twice as long as nine sections took.

He did not think about her much. He thought about the two guards he had bent on a rock in June, and about the knotter on the baler that had missed one tie in forty all through first cutting and that he had now had apart twice. He thought about the sky. It had not rained since the nineteenth of July, and after the June they'd had, a man could have wept over a sky like that.

June was the trouble. It had rained nineteen days out of thirty, and first cutting had come off three weeks late, and second cutting was late behind it. He should have been stacking by the first of August. He was starting on the thirteenth, and alfalfa wanted to be left alone for the last weeks before a hard frost so it could put something down in its roots for winter. A hard frost could come here the second week of September. Cutting in the middle of August already cost him some of next year's stand, and every day later than that cost him more of it.

He had a hundred and twenty acres to get up, at a ton and a half to the acre if it went up dry and two hundred and twenty dollars a ton, and most of it was spoken for by people who had fed his hay for years and would feed somebody else's after one musty load. The second half of the rent was due Merrill on the first of October.

It came to a little under forty thousand dollars, and for most of the next week it was going to be lying on the ground under an open sky.

He tightened the last bolt and stood up.

All right, he had thought about her some. She had walked into his barn and counted it. He had watched her do it from the floor, her lips moving and her eyes going wall to wall, and he knew counting when he saw it because he did it to a field. Then she had asked him to move four machines in the voice of a woman ordering for the table, and he had said no, and she had not backed up a step.

*Hold this,* she had said, and he had held it. She'd had dark hair coming out of its knot and her shirt stuck to her between the shoulder blades, and while she stood over that binder with her head down he had been looking at the back of her neck and had lost his place in a lease he knew by heart.

And then the phone. *Handled.* He had heard people tell a customer a thing was so before it was so. He had done it himself once, over a hundred ton of first cutting, and it had cost him the customer. She had done it with a swather twenty feet behind her.

Saturday the twenty-first, four o'clock.

On her way up to the house she had stopped and looked down at the house forty, standing green to the knee from the barn to the creek and a week from ready. She had looked at it like a lawn.

He went into the office and stood in front of the board. North down tomorrow, three days on the ground if the heat held, baled Monday. The south piece behind it. Then the house forty, the best ground he had, with a question mark he had put there himself on Sunday, the same day he shut the water off it to let the ground firm up. Cut, cure, rake, bale, haul, stack. He ran it forward from every morning he could start, and then he ran it backward from the twenty-third. He could not find a version with this barn empty on the twenty-first. In most of them the hay bay was filling.

He picked up the black marker. He thought about putting the date on the board, small, in the corner, and then he set the marker back in the tray beside the red one. He knew the date fine.

He walked out through the big door to look at the west, and the west was clean to the mountains.

The dog was not on the flatbed. She was lying out in the full sun on the bare rectangle of gravel where the rental car had been, with her chin on her paws, facing the road.

*"Clutch."*

One ear came around. The rest of her stayed where it was.
