At five on Friday morning the lights were on in his hay bay again. Eli walked over expecting more chairs.

She was carrying chairs out.

She had them two at a time, folded, one under each arm, and she was taking them from the bay through to the main floor at a fast walk. Dalton was behind her with a stack on the dolly. More than half the pallets were bare already.

Eli stood in the side door. She came back for two more and saw him, and she did not stop.

*"Lena."*

*"Dalton,"* she said, *"would you tell him the bay's his by six."*

Dalton let the dolly down. He looked at her and he looked at Eli, who was ten feet away.

*"She says the bay's yours by six."*

*"I heard her."*

*"He heard you."*

*"Thank you, Dalton."*

*"Why,"* Eli said. He said it to her.

She picked up two chairs. *"Tell him it was Wednesday for the tables, and I said so, and it's Friday. And tell him a hundred and forty people eat barbecue in there at six tonight and they'll want something to sit on. It isn't a present."*

*"She says it isn't a present."*

*"I heard."*

*"I'm supposed to be on your clock at six,"* Dalton said. *"Whose clock is this?"*

*"Mine,"* Lena said. *"I'm paying."*

*"In red,"* Dalton said, mostly to himself, and tipped the dolly back.

Eli stood aside to let her through. Then he went to the nearest stack and took two chairs off it and carried them after her.

She saw. She set hers down at table four and went back past him without a word.

*"Dalton, tell him he said he wasn't carrying anything of mine."*

Dalton drew breath to pass it on.

*"Tell her I know what I said."*

*"He knows what he said."*

On his fourth trip they met at the edge of the bay, each with a pair, and the chairs caught. A leg of hers went through a rung of his and held. For a moment they stood hip to hip, fastened together by four white chairs, with Dalton watching. Neither of them could let go without dropping something on the other's foot.

*"Lift yours,"* she said. It was the first thing she had said to him directly.

*"Which one?"*

*"The left. Your left."*

*"Your left's my right."*

*"I know that. I said yours."*

He lifted it. It came free. Her arm had been along the whole length of his for about four seconds, and it was bare to the shoulder and cool from the morning, and he went on feeling it as far as table six.

He carried thirty-some. She did not thank him and he had not thought she would. Every time they passed he could smell her hair, and every time she turned her shoulder so as not to brush his arm, and he knew to the inch how much room she was leaving. By six the bay was bare. It had forty pallets in it, and the swept boards, and one white chair standing unfolded in the middle with a back leg an inch from the gap.

She stood and looked at it. He stood and looked at it. Dalton looked at the two of them and went to find the rake.

*"That's a hundred and thirty-nine,"* Eli said.

She did not send that through anybody. She went out to her tables and left the chair where it was.

So it was his. He stood in the middle of his own bay at six in the morning with all of it back, every pallet, in time, and he felt as he had felt at nineteen on the day his father sold the roan. One of them was out. She had gone and been the one, at five in the morning, without his asking, and she had told him it was no present.

He walked the forty at half past six. It was made. He pushed his hand into a row near the top, and it was dry and sweet on the surface and still soft underneath. With tonight's dew on it at one o'clock it would bale like a dream. The strip was another thing. He knelt on it. It was cool under the top, and it bent without a sound, and it wanted another day and a half that he did not expect it to get.

The radio in the pickup had the weather where it had been. Saturday afternoon. Some play in the timing.

---

They rehearsed at noon, in his stubble.

He was at the bottom end with the rake when they came out the west door, eight or nine of them, small at that distance. The bride had her hair down. There was a tall man with one shoulder carried high, who would be the father, and a young one in a white shirt who kept hold of the bride's hand, and an older woman in sunglasses at the edge who stood with her arms folded and could only be Corinne. Lena walked backward in front of them all with the binder open on one arm.

He shut the tractor off. Nobody had asked him to. He sat on it behind the last of the creek trees with his hands hanging and watched her set a wedding out between two of his windrows.

She used the rows. He could see that from here. She stood the father and the bride at the barn and walked them down the bare stubble between a pair of doubled rows, and the rows made the sides of it. Sixty-two steps. He counted them with her. The father stopped halfway and bent, and picked up a handful of the forty, and put it to his face. The bride laughed at something. It carried all the way down.

Lena did not look toward the creek once. He knew that because he did not take his eyes off her. She had the white shirt on, the linen one. He knew how the back of her neck looked under that knot of hair from four inches off, and what it tasted like in a tractor cab, and it did him no good at all at four hundred yards with her boss standing by.

When they had gone in, he started the tractor.

Dalton found him at ten past two. He came across the stubble at a jog with his phone held out in front of him like something hot.

*"Is that supposed to be there?"*

It was the radar. There was a line on it from top to bottom, yellow with red in the middle, lying across the far side of Idaho like a rope.

*"When's that from?"*

*"Now. It says Butte by nine."* Dalton moved it with his thumb. *"It says here midnight. Midnight to two."*

Eli looked west. The sky was clean to the mountains, as it had been for thirty-two days. But the air had changed since morning. It had weight in it. He had put that down to no sleep.

He got the radio in the pickup at two-thirty. The man had lost some of his ease. *"That play in the timing we talked about,"* he said. *"Well, it's played."*

Eli sat with the door open and did it once, carefully.

There would be no baling at one. At one it would be raining. If he baled, he baled now, in the heat, bone dry, and the leaf would shatter in the chamber and blow out behind him. That was stems, cow hay, off the best stand he owned. Around five the air ahead of the front would start to come heavy. By seven the hay would take it up and be right. He would lose leaf on everything he baled before that.

That was only the baling. A bale on the ground in an inch of rain was worth nothing at all. There were better than twenty-one hundred of them to come off that field, and he had Dalton and two of Merrill's grandsons and one flatbed. Between five and midnight they might carry eight hundred and forty.

He could leave it in the row. Rained-on windrows could be turned and dried. Then it was all bleached, every ton, and he was a man selling sixty tons of cow hay off a horse field.

So he would bale from the top, by the barn, and get in what three boys could carry. It came to less than half.

The horse people bought in February. He thought he could cover one year short with a letter. He did not think he could cover it with a thousand dusty bales out from under a tarp.

*Twelve,* he had told her. *It'd stop at twelve.*

*"Get the boys,"* he said to Dalton. *"And call Kenny and ask if he's got a flatbed that runs."*

---

He was hitching the baler at half past four when she came down the yard.

She walked straight to him. She did not look around for Dalton, who was ten feet off with the twine, or for Kenny Hollenback, who had brought a second flatbed and stayed to drive it. She had her phone in her hand with the radar on it.

*"You're baling tonight."*

*"At five."*

*"It's dry. You'll lose the leaf."*

He looked at her. She had gotten that from one night in a cab.

*"Till about seven. Then it comes right."*

*"My people come at six. I'm shutting the west door and keeping them at the tables until eight. There'll be nobody in your way. And Corinne won't see a baler until she's had her dinner."*

*"She said no machinery."*

*"She said none in a sightline. A door that's shut isn't a sightline."* Something went across her face as she said it, and he thought she had heard herself. *"It's all I've got. I can't lift two thousand bales."*

*"Didn't ask you to."*

*"No. You don't."* She looked past him at the forty. *"How much do you get in?"*

*"Eight hundred. Maybe."*

*"Out of two thousand."*

*"Yeah."*

She stood there. Kenny had found something to study on his own boot. She did not say she was sorry, and Eli was glad of it, because he would not have known where to put it. She nodded once and turned to go back up.

He had the hitch pin in his hand, and he was looking at the drawbar when he spoke.

*"What happens to you Monday?"*

She stopped. *"I fly home."*

*"If she keeps you on."*

*"If I run tomorrow, yes. I fly home Monday and look for the next one."*

*"And if she doesn't."*

*"Then I fly home tomorrow."*

He dropped the pin through. *"You could stay."*

*"Stay."*

*"Here. If it goes wrong with her. Merrill wants more weddings in that barn, she's said so. There's the bunkhouse till October. You'd have something."*

He heard it as it went. So did four other people. He had asked her with his back half turned and a hand on a drawbar. He had made a man an offer of winter work in just that voice.

She was quiet for long enough that he had to straighten up and look at her.

*"If it goes wrong,"* she said.

*"I only meant—"*

*"I know what you meant. You meant it kindly."* Her voice was level and it was not loud. *"You're offering me what's left. If she fires me, if there's nowhere else, there's a bunkhouse. I told you what I do with what's left, Eli. I told you in your bed. I do breakfast. I can do that in Chicago."*

*"That's not—"*

*"You've made a place for me if I lose. You haven't said one word about wanting me here if I win."*

That was true, and he had no answer ready, and Kenny's younger boy was looking straight at him with his mouth open.

*"And you still haven't said to me what you said to her,"* Lena said. *"You said it to Corinne. I'd guess you've said it to that baler. You've just asked me to stay in Montana with a pin in your hand, looking at a hitch."*

*"Lena."*

*"No."*

She let that stand a second.

*"It's a good word. I see why you like it. Ask me when nothing's going wrong and you're looking at me. Until then it's no."*

She went back up the yard. She did not hurry. She had straw-colored dust on the backs of her boots, and they were broken in now, and he watched them all the way to the big door.

*"Well,"* Kenny said.

*"Don't."*

*"I didn't say anything,"* Dalton said.

*"You were going to."*

*"I was only going to say what my mom said."*

*"Dalton."*

*"Yes, sir."*

He started the baler at five. The hay went in dry and came out square, and behind him in the low sun a fine gray-green smoke hung over every row he took. That was the leaf. He could have priced it by the yard. He did not look back at it more than he had to.

---

Lena set a hundred and forty places.

It was a fork, a knife, a napkin folded in three, and a mason jar for a glass, a hundred and forty times, and she did it herself and did not let Dolores's girl help. She wanted something for her hands. She went around table one and table two. Through the west wall she could hear the baler. She knew that beat. She had ridden it for five hours on a pad the size of a dinner plate.

*You could stay.*

She had wanted to hear that since six on Monday morning, in a field, with his gloves on. She had not let herself want it in so many words. It had come to her over a drawbar, as a place to go if she failed, in front of Kenny Hollenback and three boys.

She laid a fork. She laid a knife.

He had meant it kindly. She could not get past that. If he had been careless she could have been angry, and anger would have carried her around fourteen tables. He had looked at what was coming for her and tried to put something under it, the only way he knew, with a roof and a season of work. It was exactly what he would have wanted offered to him. It had not crossed his mind that it was the hotel.

She laid another knife, and did table three.

*"He ask you something down there?"* Dolores said. She had come in with a tray of mason jars.

*"Who told you?"*

*"Nobody's had the time. I've got eyes. You went down that yard like a planner and you came back up it like a guest."*

Lena laid a knife. *"He asked me to stay."*

*"And?"*

*"He asked me the way you'd ask someone to watch a dog."*

Dolores set the tray down and began putting out jars behind her, one to a place. *"My husband proposed to me at a gas pump. I said no."*

*"What did he do?"*

*"He did it again at another gas pump."*

*"And then?"*

*"And then I told him where to stand,"* Dolores said.

At table nine she ran out of chairs.

There were nine at it. She stood with the tenth fork in her hand. *Every chair where a chair goes,* Corinne had said. She knew where the last one was.

The bay was swept and bare and very quiet. The pallets lay in their four rows with a hand's width between. The chair stood in the middle of them where it had stood since Wednesday. She walked out to it over the slats.

She had not folded it on Thursday morning or on Thursday night. She had left it this morning with him standing there. She put her hand on the back rail, on the place her own hands had gripped, and stood a moment. Then she folded it. It went flat with a small wooden clap. She carried it out under her arm and set it at table nine.

That made a hundred and forty.

Her phone had the forecast on it when she picked it up from the sill. There were no suns. There was a cloud with a bolt through it over tonight and another over tomorrow. She found the screenshot she had taken at the gate in Chicago, ten of them in a row, and she looked at it, and then she deleted it.

At half past five she put her shoulder to the west door and rolled it shut.

It was heavy, and it ran on an iron track, and when it closed the barn went dim and gold. She stood back and went down Corinne's list. There was a place to marry Nora that was not a hayfield, and it was this room, turned at two tomorrow and turned back in eleven minutes. Every chair was where a chair went. The board in the office was white. And there was no machine in any sightline, because she had shut the sightline.

For the first time in nine days she had it handled, and it had cost her nothing but a door.

There was a gap between two boards beside the track, about the width of a finger. She put her eye to it.

The baler was coming up the field toward the barn. It was on her sixty-two steps, or where they had been. The smoke of the leaf stood behind it in the sun. She could see him in the cab, half turned in the seat, looking back over his shoulder. She knew what that seat felt like and what the wheel felt like under her hands. She knew he was listening for one bale in forty. Farther down, two flatbeds crept along the rows, with Kenny driving one and a boy the other and the rest throwing and stacking, and she watched them for ten minutes by her phone. Between them they got twenty-four bales on. They were working as hard as people could work.

She did it in her head. She had learned how. Five a minute out of the chute. Not quite a hundred and fifty an hour onto the trucks. The bales lay behind the baler in lines, and the lines were getting longer faster than the boys were shortening them. By the creek the mountains had gone a darker blue than mountains were.

*"You want that shut for service?"* Dolores said behind her. *"It's ninety in here."*

*"Yes."*

*"They'll ask."*

*"I know they will."*

She went to the big door and stood in it with the binder on her hip. Day Nine had one line. *6 PM. WELCOME.*

At two minutes to six the first car came out of the cottonwoods. Over the mountains behind it the sky had put down a single dark line, dead level, like something ruled.
