Corinne walked the barn at ten past six with Nora on one side of her and Tom Brennan on the other, and Lena went three steps behind them with the binder, counting what they looked at.

They looked at fourteen tables. They looked at a hundred and forty forks. Corinne put her head into the office, where there was a desk and a cot and a whiteboard with nothing on it, and took her head out again. Colleen Brennan had found the loft ladder and was standing at the foot of it with a tissue already.

*"And the ceremony?"* Corinne said.

*"Here. I turn this room at two tomorrow, chairs in two blocks, and I turn it back to dinner in eleven minutes while they're having a drink under the fly."*

*"Eleven."*

*"With six people who know what they're doing."*

*"I taught you fourteen."*

*"I got it to eleven."*

Corinne nearly smiled. She looked at the west door. *"That's shut."*

*"Yes."*

*"Why?"*

The baler was going on the other side of it. It came through the boards, thump and thump. Everybody in the barn could hear it, and a hundred people were talking over it with mason jars in their hands.

*"Well,"* Corinne said, when Lena did not answer. *"It's shut. A shut door isn't a sightline. I taught you that too."* She closed her bag. *"You run tomorrow. I'll call my four at seven and tell them to unpack."*

Lena had it. She stood in the middle of her own room and had every bit of it, the job and the Saturday and the four words, and none of it had a lie in it that anyone could put a finger on.

*"What is that thumping?"* Tom Brennan said. He was a tall kind man and he had his head on one side. *"Is that a generator?"*

*It's a working ranch.* She had it ready. It was true.

*"Corinne,"* Lena said. *"Don't call them yet. Or do. I'm about to break every term you gave me."*

She put the binder on table one. She went to the west door, set both hands on the iron, and rolled it open.

The light came in first, low and orange, and then the smell. The forty lay below them all the way to the creek. A fifth of it was stubble with bales standing on it in lines, hundreds of them, and the rest was still in rows. The baler was halfway down with its smoke behind it. Two flatbeds crawled among the bales with small figures throwing. And over the mountains the whole western sky had gone the color of a gun, to a third of its height. While they watched, it lit once from inside, without a sound.

Nobody near the door said anything. The talk behind them went on a little and then began to stop.

*"Nora,"* Lena said. *"I'm going to tell you what that is, and then I'm going to ask you for something. You can say no."*

Nora had her arms folded. *"Go on."*

*"That's sixty tons of hay. It's the best he grows. He sells it to people with horses, and it pays his bank, and it's the only hay he puts under a roof. It goes in that bay through there. There's a storm coming at midnight, and a bale that gets rained on is worth nothing. He has three boys and a neighbor. They'll get in less than half."* She kept her voice where Nora's was. *"He didn't spring the cutting on me. I let all of you think so yesterday. I worked it out on Tuesday, before he did it. I knew. And there's a strip across the middle that won't cure in time, and that one's mine. I had his water turned on for your photograph."*

Behind Nora, Corinne's face did not move at all.

*"I can't fix it,"* Lena said. *"I've said handled to you for nine days and I can't lift one of those twice. I want to ask your guests to carry hay. Tonight, in what they've got on. If they do, it goes in that bay up to the roof, and tomorrow you're married in this room in front of a wall of it. Corinne has told me that's a storeroom. She isn't wrong."*

*"And if I say no?"*

*"Then I shut that door and you have a lovely dinner, and tomorrow there's a gray drape where the hay would have been. And he loses it."*

*"Why are you asking me?"*

*"Because they're your guests. And because the last time I decided what you could stand to hear, I sent you a picture taken from somebody's knees."*

Nora looked down the field for a while. She was a nurse. Lena watched her do what she must do forty times a shift, and it did not take her long.

*"How heavy is one?"*

*"Sixty pounds."*

*"Dad's got a shoulder."*

*"Then he counts. Somebody has to count."*

*"I can count,"* Tom Brennan said. *"I fell out of that loft and I can still count."*

*"Okay,"* Nora said. She unfolded her arms. *"Where do I stand?"*

*"Lena."* It was Corinne, very low.

*"I know."*

*"That is my goddaughter's wedding you're proposing to fill with hay."*

*"Yes."*

Corinne looked at her for a long moment. Then she looked at the field, and the sky, and the three boys.

*"That's the cufflink,"* she said. *"I'll call nobody until I've seen whether a hundred and forty people in silk will pick up a bale for a man they've never met."*

There was a chair at table nine with nobody in it. Lena carried it to the middle of the floor and got up on it. She had never done that at a wedding in her life. Her whole trade was that nobody saw her.

*"I'm Lena. I'm the planner. I need to ask you all for something, and it isn't on the invitation."*

She told them in six sentences. She did not make it pretty. When she got to the end the barn was quiet enough to hear the baler, and a hundred and forty people looked at her and at their shoes.

*"Where do you want us?"* somebody said.

It was the best man. He already had his jacket off and his cuffs turned back. There was always one. Five more in the same tie got up from table six behind him.

*"I'll telephone,"* Mrs. Hollenback said from the big door, to nobody, and went up to the house faster than Lena had ever seen her go.

After that it was a seating plan.

She had done harder ones. She put a line from the side door of the bay to its back wall, shoulder to shoulder, tall ones at the far end. She put the six groomsmen and anybody under thirty on the trucks. Anyone with a hip or a heart went to the door with Mr. Brennan to count. *"If your shoes cost more than a bale of hay, take them off or ruin them. I won't ask which."* She gave Corinne the binder, open at Day Nine, and a pen.

*"By tens,"* she said.

*"I can't remember when I was last handed a clipboard,"* Corinne said. She took her shoes off and stood them side by side under table one.

Then Lena walked down the field.

The stubble was sharp through her soles. The baler came up to meet her with its lights on, and she stood where he would have to stop, and he stopped. She went up the three steps. He opened the cab door. He had dust caked in the sweat on his face and his eyes were raw, and he was looking past her at the barn. A line of people in wedding clothes was coming out of it.

*"What did you do?"*

*"I asked."*

He looked at her then. The baler thumped behind them.

*"I said I'd tell you and I didn't,"* he said.

He said it to her face from two feet away, and then he shut his mouth.

*"I know. I turned your water on and went and talked on the phone."*

*"I know."*

*"Where do you want them?"*

*"Lena."*

*"It'll keep. I've got a hundred and forty people. Tell me where they go."*

He told her. The first two hours' bales were stemmy and went on top, where he could get them out for cows. The strip stayed on the ground. The corners of the stack had to be tied in or it would lean. Wyatt knew how.

*"Is Wyatt coming?"* he said.

*"Merrill's on the telephone."*

*"She's what?"*

*"She's making up for July."*

---

He baled.

He had never done it with a crowd watching. Now when he looked over his shoulder there were people the length of the field. They came down the rows in pairs in the last of the light, women with their skirts tied up at the hip and men in dress shirts gone dark, and they took the bales as the chute dropped them. After the first half hour there was nothing lying behind him at all.

The air came right at ten to seven. He felt it in the back of his neck first and then he heard it, the rattle going out of the hay. He got down and twisted a handful. It bent and held.

Headlights came up the cottonwood drive and kept coming. Wyatt's trailer was the first. Then Cash Hollander, Wyatt's brother-in-law, came with a stock trailer and his boy, and his wife Deb with him, who had been pulling calves for twenty years and set a bale on the fifth tier as if it were a feed sack. June McClaren was in a sundress and Wyatt's work gloves. The caps from Teagan's came, all five, in one pickup. Kenny's wife came. Dalton's mother came, and Eli had never seen her before, and she looked hard at him and then at Lena. She nodded like someone closing a ledger.

He could hear the count. It came down the field every few minutes in the voice of a tall man with a bad shoulder. *"Four hundred!"* And a cheer, ragged, all up the line.

They handled it right. That was what he could not get over. They took each bale by the strings and set it down on its edge, as somebody had plainly shown them at the door. The bride went past his cab with her hair tied up in a napkin and a bale against her thighs, and the groom behind her with two. They were strangers from Chicago, in the dark, and they were being careful of his hay.

She was at the bay door. He could pick her out from the bottom of the field under the yard light, by how she stood. She had one arm up, pointing. A hundred and forty people looked where she pointed. He had seen her do it to five.

Once, at the top of a round, he passed close enough to see into the bay. The stack was at six tiers and going up in steps. The line ran along it hand to hand, in at the side door and up, and there was Corinne in her stocking feet at the door with a binder, making a mark for every tenth bale. At ten o'clock she handed the binder to the tall man and took a place in the line herself. She had put her shoes back on to do it, and they were good shoes. Wyatt was on the stack tying corners. Up on the highest tier, moving up a step each time the hay did, was the McClaren hen.

Every fortieth bale or so the knotter missed. The broken ones were carried off to one side, and Deacon had let himself out to see about them.

By half past ten the lightning had sound to it. He finished the last full row at twenty past eleven and stopped at the top of the strip.

She was there. She had come down from the door. She had hay in her hair, and his gloves on.

*"That's all of it,"* he said. *"All but this."*

*"Can it go in?"*

*"It's tough. Stack it tight and it heats. It'd take the rest with it."*

*"How long will a tough bale keep by itself, with air around it?"*

*"On its own? A week. It's stacking that does it."*

*"Then bale it. I'll take the whole hundred and thirty. I won't put them in your stack. I want them on my floor."*

*"What for?"*

*"It's my aisle,"* she said. *"I watered it."*

He baled the strip. It went in heavy and came out dark. The best man and his five carried it up the slope two bales at a time, in through the west door, and he could see her under the string lights with her arm out, showing them where to set it down.

The first drops hit the cab glass at two minutes to midnight. The wipers were off. He left them off.

*"Nineteen hundred and ninety-eight!"* the tall man shouted from the bay door, and then, when the cheer had gone down, *"The mule ate two!"*

It came down in earnest at ten past. By then the west door was shut. The side door was shut too, with sixty tons behind it. A hundred and forty people stood in a barn with hay down their collars eating brisket with their hands.

Eli came in last. He stood inside the big door with rain running off his hat. A woman in gray linen with hay down the front of it came across the floor to him, and she held out her hand, and he took it.

*"Nineteen hundred and ninety-eight,"* Corinne said. *"I counted them. I have sent my four back to bed."* She turned to Lena. *"That wall is a storeroom. I'd like it lit from below."*

---

The last shuttle left at one. Dalton flagged it down the drive in the rain with a staple gun, because that was what he had in his hand.

Lena turned the room alone. She had told everybody to go, and they had gone, and she had not noticed whether that included him. She was too tired to stop, and there was a wedding in fifteen hours. The tables went to the walls with their cloths on. The chairs went into two blocks of seventy, facing the bay.

There was no bay to see anymore. The opening was full to the top. It was a wall of bales forty-four feet long and thirteen high, tight and square. Wyatt had tied the corners. It was green, a deep soft green with gray in it. She set the uplights along the foot of it at three in the morning and ran the cable to the generator, and switched them on to test them.

She stood back.

It was greener than the photograph. It was greener than June. It went up into the dark of the roof with the light sliding over every bale. The whole barn smelled of it, sweet and heavy, like a field at night with rain on tin over the top. Down the middle of the floor, from the big door to the foot of the wall, ran her aisle. There were dark bales on each side of it, two deep, with a hand's width between for the air.

She went into the office for the staple gun.

The board was not white.

There was one line on it. It was at the top, in black, in his capitals, larger than he had ever written anything.

*SAT 21. 4 PM. WEDDING.*

She stood in front of that for some time.

*"I was going to put it up Thursday,"* he said behind her. *"The first one. The twelfth. I had the marker out."*

He was in the door. He had not gone anywhere. He had been outside in the rain putting the swather and the tractor and the baler around the back of the pole building, one after another, where no one coming up the drive would see them. He was soaked to the skin.

*"Why didn't you?"*

*"I knew the date fine."*

She laughed. It came out cracked. He crossed the office and she let him. He stood in front of her and did not touch her.

*"Show me your hands,"* he said.

She pulled the gloves off. They had not been enough. Both palms were open across the pads. He held them by the wrists and turned them to the light, and then he showed her his. They were worse. He had been in and out of the knotter all night, and there was twine burn across every finger.

*"My father's baler,"* he said. *"I never told you that."*

*"No."*

*"He died in March of 'sixteen, the week the lease came due. I signed it in his place. I went out the morning after to start the tractor, and there was a note on the seat. He'd written it that week."*

*"What did it say?"*

*"'Knotter misses one in forty. Don't fix it, count it.'"*

*"That's all?"*

*"That's all. I've had it apart twice a year since."*

*"Thirty-eight,"* she said. *"The first one on Sunday night was thirty-eight."*

*"I know. I thought, she's counting it. He'd have liked that."* His voice had gone. He waited for it.

She turned her hands over in his and held on, as well as she could.

*"You said it tonight,"* she said. *"With a baler going. That's twice now, once to my boss and once over a machine."*

*"Third time I'll get somewhere quiet."*

They went out onto the floor. He stopped at the top of the aisle and looked down it at the wall, with the light coming up it, and she watched him see his own hay.

*"You asked,"* he said.

*"I asked."*

*"I'd have lost more than half of it before I asked anybody for anything."*

*"I know you would."*

*"Thank you."*

There was a pile of gray table runners by the wall that she had not found a use for. They lay down on it because a ladder was beyond both of them. It was not hay. It was linen, and it smelled of the steamer.

Neither of them could work a button. She tried his and her fingers would not close. He tried hers. They gave up and laughed about it quietly, with their foreheads together, and pulled things over heads and pushed them down with the heels of their hands. She had to open the binder's zipper pocket with her teeth.

*"Eight,"* he said. *"There were nine."*

*"That's five of my six groomsmen accounted for."*

*"Which was the one?"*

*"The best man. He was asleep on the last shuttle."*

They lay on their sides, face to face. She put her leg over his hip and he came into her slowly, a little at a time, and that was all the strength either had. It was hardly a movement. It was more of a rocking, close, with his mouth against her forehead and her sore hands flat on his back. The rain went on overhead. The light came up the hay and down over them.

*"All right?"* he said.

*"Don't stop. Don't hurry."*

*"I couldn't do either."*

*"That's good,"* she whispered. *"Stay deep. I want all of you in me and I can't lift a thing to get it."*

*"Tell me where."*

*"Tilt me. Up. There. Right there, Eli, keep your cock right there."*

*"I've got you."*

He listened as he always did. He kissed the inside of each of her wrists above the torn place. She felt it build slowly, from a long way off, with nothing to push against but him, and when it came she only held on tighter and breathed his name into his neck. He followed her, very quietly, and he shook all down his back.

They did not move apart.

*"Dew's on,"* Lena said.

*"That's rain."*

*"Is that good?"*

*"It's right."*

Dolores had left a brisket sandwich in foil on table one with *LENA* on it in pencil. They ate it in two halves, lying down.

At six it was gray in the gaps of the siding. She got up and turned off the lights along the wall, to save them for four o'clock.
