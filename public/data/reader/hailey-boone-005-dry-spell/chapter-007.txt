Eli said *yeah* at five o'clock on Tuesday afternoon, and he meant it for about seven hours.

He had the arithmetic done before the man on the radio got to the low of fifty-four. It was not hard arithmetic. The storm was Saturday afternoon. The forty had to be baled in the dew on Friday night and under the roof by Saturday noon, and hay wanted three days on the ground, and three days back from Friday night was Wednesday morning. He had lain there with her head on his arm and felt her get to the same place, about two breaths behind him, and stop.

Then she had asked him, and he had said it. He would tell her. He would tell her before she left.

She left at six. She had Bea at six-thirty, about the second batch of roses. He stood on the porch with it ready, *I'm cutting the forty at daylight,* and she turned around at the car and looked back at him with her whole face open. She had looked at him like that for one second in the office on Saturday, and he had liked it better than anything she said. He could not say it into that. She lifted her hand, and he lifted his.

*"Tonight, then,"* he told the dog.

He raked the five bleached rows at nine. At ten he greased the swather by the trouble light, every fitting on it, and he did not look too closely at what that meant. A man who greased a swather at ten at night had made up his mind about the morning. At a quarter to twelve he got in the pickup and drove to Bridger Creek.

The motel had a sign that said VACANCY with the V out. Her rental was nosed in at number six. There was an ice machine beside her door, lit blue, and a light behind her curtain. He parked across the lot and shut the engine off.

He had the six words. He went over what came after them.

She would not shout. He knew that much about her now. She would stand in the door with her arms folded and count, and then she would ask him for something reasonable. *One day. Cut it Thursday night, after she's seen it standing. Let her walk down it once.* She would say it in the low voice from the bunkhouse, the one that had said *I'll do breakfast.*

And he would give it to her.

He sat with both hands on the wheel and looked at that. He had said no to her on the twelfth of August in under four seconds, with a grease gun in his hand. He had been good at it all his life. He could not have done it tonight to save the stand, and he knew to the hour when he had lost the knack. It was about two on Monday morning, with the wipers going.

One day was the forty. Cut on Thursday night it had Friday to cure and nothing more, and he would be baling green hay ahead of a storm, hay that heated in the bale and came out gray with dust at Christmas. That was eleven years of horse people. That was the bank, and the thirteenth March.

So she would not be unfair to him. She would be fair, and he would say yes, and he would lose it.

The ice machine dropped a load of ice. Her light went out.

He sat there until one. Then he drove back and baled five rows in the dew, a hundred and four bales, and stacked them by hand in the dark to give his arms something to do.

At twenty to five he went into the office. Her red was where it had been since Friday. *DO NOT CUT.* He wiped off the question mark with the heel of his hand, the black and the red together, and wrote in the place where it had been.

*HOUSE 40. Cut Wed 18.*

He looked at it. He had told her she would hear it from him before she read it up there. He had pointed at this board with this marker when he said it.

There were lights on in the hay bay when he brought the swather around at ten past five. Her car was in the yard, one space left of the dog, and Dalton's truck was beside it. She had said five o'clock for the tables, and here she was at five o'clock. He could see the two of them through the side door, her at one end of an eight-footer and the kid at the other.

He could have stopped. It was thirty feet out of his way. He could have opened the cab door and said it over the engine, with the kid standing there, and it would have been late and badly done and still his own mouth.

He drove past the door. She came to it with her end of the table in her hands and stood and watched him go by. He saw her in the mirror. She did not call out, and she did not put the table down. She had known since five yesterday. He had felt her know it.

He stopped at the top of the field, where her sixty-two steps began, and lowered the header.

It was the best stand he had ever grown. It came into the reel thick and even and went down behind him in a row as fat as a man's body, and it smelled like every good year he'd had. He cut her aisle on the first pass. He did not watch it go. Fifty yards down, the header crossed onto the strip she had watered, and he felt the drive wheels sink and take hold. When he came back up on the second round there were two dark ruts across it, a hand deep.

That strip would be a day behind the rest. It would not be fit to bale on Friday night. It was two and a half acres of the best hay he had, about a hundred and thirty bales, and it was going to lie out under whatever came on Saturday unless he baled it tough and kept it apart. He had known that at the motel too.

The sun came over the mountains on the third round and lit the cut rows from the side. He had always liked that. A field looked its best to him in the first hour it was down, striped, with the dew still on the stubble. He could not see this one for the sixty-two steps at the top of it. He kept finding the place with his eyes every time he turned.

He made the fourth round, and the fifth. Around the tenth he caught himself talking, out loud, in the cab. *I said I'd tell you.* He had the rest of it. It was no use to anybody in a swather.

*"He's cutting the forty,"* Dalton said.

*"Yes."*

*"He never said he was doing the forty today."*

*"No."*

*"It's on the board, though. I saw it when I came through for the dolly. I figured you knew."*

*"I knew,"* Lena said.

She had his gloves on. She had put them on at five for the blisters, without thinking about whose they were, and she had been carrying tables in them for ten minutes when the swather came around the corner of the barn. She had stood in the door and watched the cab go by. He had been looking straight ahead. He had looked straight ahead so carefully that she knew he had seen her.

She was still holding her end of the table. It was an eight-footer and it weighed about what a bale weighed, and she had been standing in the door of the bay with it since the swather went by. She backed up. They carried it across the main floor and leaned it against the north wall.

Then she went into the office and read it.

*HOUSE 40. Cut Wed 18.* It was in his capitals, and the question mark was gone from beside it. He had taken her red off along with his black. A foot to the right, in her own hand, the board still said *DO NOT CUT.*

She had worked it out herself on Tuesday, on his arm, in about four seconds. She had driven out here in the dark this morning knowing what she would hear when the sun came up. She had heard it, and it had gone through her all the same. The cut was arithmetic, and she could do arithmetic. What she had turned over all night in number six, with the ice machine going, was *yeah.*

She uncapped the red marker. Her hand was perfectly steady.

*I READ IT ON THE BOARD.*

She put the cap back on. She took off his gloves, one and then the other, and laid them on the desk under the board with the fingers together. Then she went and got another table with her bare hands.

They had all forty on the main floor by a quarter past six. Dalton worked like a boy who wanted very much to be somewhere else and was too well raised to say so. At six he had looked at his watch, and then at her.

*"Go on,"* she said, when the last one was down. *"What did he leave you?"*

Dalton took a folded page out of his shirt pocket. *"Pallets in the bay when the tables are out. Forty, in four rows. Then the rake."*

*"He wrote you a note."*

*"He writes me one every day. He doesn't talk a lot in the morning."*

*"Or the evening."*

Dalton considered whether he was allowed to agree with that. *"He talks to the dog,"* he said, and went to get the pallets.

Lena set the room.

It took her all morning, and she did not think while she did it. That was what the work was for. She put fourteen rounds on the tape. She took the linen out of its boxes and steamed it on the table with the travel steamer from the side pocket of the binder, the ivory first and then the gray runner. The loft rail got its first hundred feet of lights. The swather went around and around outside the west door, getting farther off and coming back.

She did not look out the west door.

*"Well, now,"* Mrs. Hollenback said from the big door at nine. She had the clipboard and a plate. She stood and took in the fourteen tables, and the lights, and she put one hand flat on her apron. *"I've had a dance in here every summer since 1976. I never once saw it look like that."*

*"Thank you."*

*"He's cutting the house piece."*

*"Yes."*

*"Did he say?"*

*"He wrote it down."*

Mrs. Hollenback looked at the office door for a while. She was not delighted. Lena had not seen her not delighted before, and it made her look her age.

*"His father was the same,"* she said. *"He'd fix your fence at night and leave you a bill in the mailbox. You'd have paid him double to knock."* She set the plate on table one, in front of the bride's grandmother. *"That's a compliment about the room, honey. I don't know what the other is."*

At ten she took the steamer out to the yard spigot to fill it, and her phone found its two bars and buzzed. NORA. *Still on for 3 your time?? Mom is going to watch too. She bought a ring light.*

It was the video walk-through. Lena had written it on Day Seven in her own hand. *3 PM. NORA BY VIDEO. START AT WEST DOOR. WALK THE AISLE.*

She went to the west door then.

Half of it was down. From the barn to well past the wheel line it lay in long gray-green rows on yellow stubble, and the swather was a small thing working along the edge of what was left. Her line was gone. The place where the ground leveled was gone. There were two ruts across the field where the strip had been, and they were hers.

She could stand in this door at three o'clock and hold up her phone and show it to Nora Brennan and her mother and a ring light. She could say it. *It's a hay field and it has been cut.* It was nine words.

She typed it once.

*Change of plan, let's skip the video. You're here tomorrow and I'd rather you saw it in person. It's worth the wait.*

*OK!!* Nora sent. *You're the boss.*

Lena stood there with that. Then she went back to the linen.

The chair truck came a little before noon. It was a different outfit, a man and his son out of Bozeman who did chairs and nothing else. They had a hundred and forty, white, wooden, folding, in stacks of ten.

*"Where do you want them?"* the father said.

She knew where she had wanted them. Day Seven said *CHAIRS: N. WALL, MAIN FLOOR, UNTIL SAT A.M.* On Saturday morning they went out to the meadow in two blocks of seventy. The north wall had ten buffet tables and fourteen cocktail tables stacked along it now. The meadow was in rows.

She looked at the hay bay.

Dalton had done it right. There were forty pallets on the swept boards, in four rows of ten, square to the wall, with a hand's width between them for the air. It was the tidiest thing on the place. It was waiting for something, and she knew what. She had given her word about this room on Saturday, to a man with a marker in his hand. *Wednesday for the tables.* She had kept it by a quarter past six.

*Under cover,* the chair contract said, *and off the ground.*

*"In there,"* she said. *"On the pallets."*

Dalton had come up from the yard with a grease gun. He stopped.

*"Those are for the forty."*

*"I know what they're for."*

*"He said nothing else goes in there."*

*"He said a lot of things."*

Dalton looked at the chair man, and at the chair man's son, who was about his own age, and got no help from either. *"I work for the black one,"* he said, unhappily.

*"Then go and tell the black one."*

He looked out at the swather. It was a long way off. He looked at Lena. Then he went and greased the rake with great attention, with his back to the bay, and he did not tell anybody anything.

It took the two men twenty minutes. Fourteen stacks of ten went onto Eli Brandt's pallets, white on raw pine, one stack to a pallet, in two lines from the side door to the back wall. They did not fill the room. They only made sure nothing else could be put in it. When the truck had gone she stood and looked at it. She had done it in the voice she used on caterers.

She walked down the middle of it once. The stacks came to her hip. With the side door open the light fell in across them from the east, and it was a clean, plain, high room, and some part of her that never stopped working noted that it would seat a hundred and forty with an aisle. She told that part to be quiet.

The hen came in under the side door, walked the length of the bay, and flew up onto the nearest stack.

*"Don't,"* Lena said. *"I know."*

She went into the office and wrote it down, since that was how things got said on this place.

*140 CHAIRS. HAY BAY.*

---

He finished at twenty to four.

The whole forty lay behind him. He shut the swather off at the bottom, by the creek, and sat with the door open and the engine ticking. It was forty acres in windrows, as even as he had ever laid them, and sixty-four tons. He had not eaten since yesterday or slept since Monday. He looked at it and felt nothing he could use.

From down here he could see the barn as she had meant the bride to see it, only backward. The west door stood open at the top of the slope. There was a string of lights along the loft inside. Somebody small went across the doorway with her arms full of something white.

He walked up through the rows, and then up the strip. His own ruts were in it. The hay on either side of them lay heavy and dark, and when he put his hand under it the ground was cool.

Clutch was lying across the side door of the hay bay, half in and half out. She did not get up. He stepped over her.

He stood there for some time.

There were a hundred and forty white chairs on his pallets. They went back in two lines to the wall, and somebody had squared every stack. On top of the nearest one, at about the height of his belt, the McClaren hen sat looking up at him.

*"Don't,"* he said.

He went through toward the office, and the main floor stopped him on the way. He had not seen it set. It had fourteen round tables on it, with cloths down to the boards and a gray stripe across each, and the light came through the siding and lay over all of them, and there were small lamps along the loft. He had kept machinery in this barn for twelve years. He had not known it could be made to look like that. It looked like a place where a person would want to be married. He stood at the edge of it in his boots, with chaff down his neck, and did not walk on it.

There was red on the board.

*I READ IT ON THE BOARD.*

He read that one twice. Below it, beside the smear, was the other.

*140 CHAIRS. HAY BAY.*

His gloves were on the desk. They were laid side by side, palms down, and squared to the edge like everything she set. He looked at them before he looked at either line again.

He picked up the black marker. He was tired enough that the board moved a little.

*CHAIRS OUT.*

He stood there with the cap off. It was not what he had come in to write. He had the words from the cab, and he had them in the right order. He looked at *I READ IT ON THE BOARD,* and he could not see where on a whiteboard a man put a thing like that.

He heard her on the main floor. She came to the office door and stopped in it. She had a staple gun in one hand and her hair tied up in a strip of the gray linen. She read what he had written. She came in past him, close, without touching him, as he had gone past her on Saturday, and she took the red.

*HAY OUT.*

He wrote under it. *IT'S MY BAY.*

She wrote under that. *IT WAS MY AISLE.*

The markers squeaked. Neither of them had said a word. She smelled of steam and clean cotton. She had a burn across one wrist, and her eyes were red at the rims, and so, he supposed, were his. He had the cap off still. There was room under her line. He could have put it there, eight words, in black, and she would have read it standing a foot away from him.

She waited. He saw her wait.

*"Lena,"* he said.

*"No,"* she said. *"Not on that."*

She capped her marker and laid it in the tray, and she went back out to her tables.

He could have carried the chairs out. It was a hundred and forty chairs and it would have taken him an hour. He had told her on Saturday that he was not carrying anything of hers. He went through to the bay. He lifted the hen off the nearest stack and set her on the next one. He took the top chair and unfolded it in the middle of his own pallets, and sat down.

It was a good chair. He sat in it and looked at the other hundred and thirty-nine until the light went off the wall.
